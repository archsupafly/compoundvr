[Pavlov](/games/pavlov) has been called "Counter-Strike in VR" since its 2017 Early Access launch, but that nickname undersells what it became. CS kept the same handful of maps and modes for years. Pavlov handed the keys to its community through the Steam Workshop, and the community built the game Vankrupt's core design only gestured at.

The Workshop turned Pavlov into a bottomless multiplayer sandbox. Custom maps, remade Counter-Strike classics, a whole Trouble in Terrorist Town scene, zombies survival, custom game modes, and a library of community-built weapons. Here are the Pavlov mods that matter.

## The Custom Maps: Where the Real Catalog Lives

The heart of Pavlov's mod scene is its map library. Because the game's gunplay and movement were built for VR, community maps are where the multiplayer actually happens — and the best of them are direct ports of Counter-Strike's legendary layouts.

The essential downloads are the **Counter-Strike map ports**. Dust II, Dust, de_basalt, the classics — rebuilt room-for-room in Pavlov's engine so you can finally walk the middle of Dust with a real weapon in your hands, physically crouch behind the crates, and peak the angle with your actual head. These aren't pastiche; they're faithful remakes tuned for VR movement. For anyone who grew up on CS, walking through a remade de_dust2 in a headset is the single most powerful Pavlov moment there is.

Beyond the CS ports, the Workshop's map library is enormous and wildly varied — community-designed arenas, sci-fi facilities, warehouses, courtyard shooters, and maps built specifically for the modes below. The map list is always turning over, so browsing the [Pavlov Workshop](https://steamcommunity.com/app/555160/workshop/) sorted by popularity is the reliable way to find what's current.

## Trouble in Terrorist Town: Pavlov's Signature Mode

The most important Pavlov mod isn't a map — it's a game mode. **Trouble in Terrorist Town** (TTT) took over Pavlov in a way no other community mode has, and it's the reason many people buy the game.

TTT is the social-deduction shooter from Garry's Mod: a group of players starts a round, some are innocent, some are traitors, and nobody knows who's who. The traitors secretly kill, the innocents hunt them by reading the bodies and the behavior, and every round ends in accusations, betrayals, and violence. In VR, with actual hands and actual voice proximity, TTT is a completely different beast — the physical tells, the real conversations, the tension of trusting the wrong person across a room. The custom TTT maps are the centerpiece: everything from small arenas built for deduction to elaborate set pieces like the Bioshock-style Rapture map, where the atmosphere does half the work.

## Zombies: Co-op Survival Done Right

Pavlov's **Zombies mode** — and its map collections — is the other pillar of the mod scene. It launched with the game's update lineup and immediately spawned a catalog of survival maps where you and a team fight endless waves of shamblers using Pavlov's manual-reload shooting.

The zombies maps play like the best of both worlds: the tension of keeping your weapon chambered and your magazine full under pressure, combined with the spatial terror of zombies rounding a corner you can't see. Nuketown and the COD-style zombie maps brought that legacy structure into VR, and the mode became one of Pavlov's most consistent sources of populated lobbies. It's also the best place for Pavlov's more casual audience — no economy, no clutch pressure, just shooting and surviving with friends.

## Custom Modes: Infection, Parkour, and the Framework Modders

Beyond maps, the community built whole game modes. **Infection** — one player starts infected, tags others, last human alive wins — translates perfectly to Pavlov's physical shooter and became a community staple. **Parkour** maps test your movement in a shooter that doesn't have native wall-running, and the community made it work anyway.

Underneath all of it sits the modding framework. Tools like **Pavlov PMF** (the Pavlov Modding Framework) gave creators a real pipeline for building custom modes on top of Pavlov's structure. That's why the Workshop has weird, wonderful stuff beyond straight remakes — bespoke game modes that turn Pavlov into something its developer never built. The framework is the reason the scene could do more than port maps.

## What to Know Before You Dive In

A few honest notes. Pavlov's mod scene lives on **PCVR via the Steam Workshop** — that's where the full catalog is. The Quest port (Pavlov Shack) has a separate, smaller content pipeline, and you don't get the same Workshop library. So if the mods matter to you, PCVR is the place to be.

Also worth knowing: because the map list rotates constantly and new modes drop all the time, the "best mods" are a moving target. The constants are the CS ports, the TTT maps, and the Zombies survival maps. Everything else is a browse-by-popularity situation.

## Is It Worth It?

If you're playing Pavlov for the base Search & Destroy loop, the mods are the game's true shelf life. The Workshop turns a good VR shooter into a bottomless multiplayer sandbox — remade classics, a killer social-deduction mode in TTT, wave survival in Zombies, and a custom-mode scene built on a real modding framework.

It's the mods that made Pavlov more than "Counter-Strike in VR." They made it the one VR shooter where the community decided what the game was, and the community decided it was a whole library of games. Install the Workshop maps, find a TTT lobby, and you'll understand why Pavlov is still filling lobbies years after its Early Access launch.

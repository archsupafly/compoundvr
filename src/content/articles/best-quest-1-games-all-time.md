The original Oculus Quest launched on May 21, 2019 with 53 games — a lineup that included Beat Saber, Superhot VR, and a lot of confidence that standalone VR could work. Two years later, Meta had shipped the Quest 2 and moved on. But the Quest 1 didn't die when its successor arrived. It kept getting games. Developers kept supporting it. And by the time support started thinning out in late 2022, the little headset that could had built a library that punched way above its weight class.

This is the full story of that library. Twenty games that justified the hardware — the store exclusives that sold headsets, the sideloaded ports that proved the community could do what Meta wouldn't, and the cross-platform titles that ran just fine on the Snapdragon 835 with its 72Hz OLED and 1440×1600 per eye. No PC required. No wires. Just the headset and your hands.

The Quest 1 was never going to match the Quest 2's resolution, refresh rate, or Snapdragon XR2 muscle. But it had something the Quest 2 never quite captured: the sense that you were holding the future in your hands for the first time. Every game on this list earned its place by proving that future was real.

## #1: Beat Saber

![Beat Saber](/images/games/beat-saber-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

[Beat Saber](/games/beat-saber) was already the best VR game when the Quest launched. It had been the killer app on PC-VR since 2018 — the rhythm-slicing game that made VR fun for people who didn't think they'd like VR. On the Quest, it became essential.

The magic was the cable. Or rather, the absence of it. On the Rift, Beat Saber was excellent but tethered — you swung your arms knowing a cable ran from your head to a PC, a constant reminder that this was still a peripheral, not a standalone experience. On the Quest, the cable was gone. You ducked, you sidestepped, you swung with full commitment and zero tether. The room-scale tracking kept up. The graphics were slightly softer than the PC version. You didn't care.

Beat Saber was the game you showed people who'd never tried VR. It was the game that made non-gamers understand why headsets existed. And on the Quest, it was the game that proved the hardware was ready for the software that mattered. The DLC pipeline kept it fresh for years, and the custom song community on PC made it infinite. On Quest, you bought it from the store and it just worked — no modding, no setup, no troubleshooting. That was the whole point of the hardware, and Beat Saber delivered on that promise better than anything else in the library.

## #2: Resident Evil 4 VR

![Resident Evil 4 VR](/images/games/resident-evil-4-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

Armature Studio took one of the greatest action games ever made and rebuilt it for VR from the ground up — not a port with a headset toggle, but a genuine rethinking of how the game plays when you're standing inside it. [Resident Evil 4 VR](/games/resident-evil-4) arrived in October 2021 as the Quest's late masterpiece, the system seller that arrived after everyone assumed the hardware was done getting heavy hitters.

The motion controls change everything. Leon's handgun becomes a real weapon in your right hand — you aim down the sights, you squeeze the trigger, you feel the kick. The inventory system moves to a wrist-mounted grid you can access without pausing, which keeps the action flowing when eight Ganados are closing in from different angles. The knife becomes a physical object you swing, blocking incoming attacks with timing that rewards skill over button-mashing.

It's still recognizably the 2005 classic — the village, the castle, the island, the slow reveal of what's really going on. But in VR, the horror hits different. When a chainsaw man charges you in the opening village sequence, you don't press a button to dodge. You physically lean back. You feel the wind. And when you survive, the relief is real in a way no screen can replicate. Resident Evil 4 was already a perfect game. In VR, it found another gear entirely.

## #3: SUPERHOT VR

![SUPERHOT VR](/images/games/superhot-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

[SUPERHOT VR](/games/superhot-vr) is the game that answers the question "what is VR actually for?" Time moves when you move. Bullets hang in the air as frozen red streaks. You lean around them, pluck a pistol off a dead enemy, and shatter the next guy with a thrown mug. Every level is a puzzle you solve with your body, and every solution feels like something you'd see in a movie — except you did it, and your heart is hammering, and you're sweating through your shirt.

On the Quest, SUPERHOT VR became the purest expression of what standalone VR could be. No cable to trip over during the frantic moments. No PC to worry about. Just you, the room, and the slow-motion ballet of dodging bullets and improvising weapons. The game is short — you can finish it in two or three hours — but the replay value is in the mastery. Each level has dozens of solutions, and the satisfaction of finding a cleaner, faster, more stylish path never gets old.

The free Quest 2 upgrade sharpened the visuals, but the Quest 1 version was already the definitive standalone experience. It was the game that made people who thought VR was a gimmick suddenly understand why someone would strap a computer to their face. That's a rare trick, and SUPERHOT VR pulled it off with nothing but white environments, red enemies, and the beautiful simplicity of moving through a world that waits for you.

## #4: Pistol Whip

![Pistol Whip](/images/games/pistol-whip-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

Cloudhead Games shipped [Pistol Whip](/games/pistol-whip) on the Quest launch day, and it's the game that proved rhythm-action could work as a full-body experience. You move through stylized levels, shooting to the beat, dodging bullets Matrix-style, never reloading, always flowing. It's pure kinetic joy — the kind of game that makes you feel like an action hero even when you're missing half your shots.

The core loop is simple: enemies appear in time with the music, you shoot them, you dodge their return fire, you keep moving. The scoring rewards staying on beat, which turns what could be a simple gallery shooter into a genuine rhythm game where your body is the instrument. The gunplay feels weighty and satisfying, and the level design encourages movement — you're not standing still, you're weaving through a neon-lit action movie.

The 2089 campaign expansion turned a rhythm-shooter into a proper revenge story, adding narrative stakes to the kinetic spectacle. But even without the expansion, Pistol Whip was the game that made Quest owners feel like their headset could deliver experiences that weren't just "good for VR." They were just good. Period.

## #5: Moss

![Moss](/images/games/moss-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

[Moss](/games/moss) is the game that proved VR didn't have to be about shooting things. Polyarc's fairy tale puts you in the role of areader — a god-like presence watching over Quill, a tiny mouse with a sword and a dream. You guide her through a gorgeous miniature world, solving puzzles, fighting enemies, and occasionally reaching in with your spectral hands to move obstacles or heal her when she takes damage.

The magic is the scale. Quill is small enough that you lean down to talk to her, and she looks up at you with an expression that makes you feel responsible for her. When she's hurt, you reach in to help. When she's scared, you're there. It's an intimacy that no flat-screen game can match — the feeling that you're not just controlling a character, but protecting one.

The Quest version runs beautifully on the Snapdragon 835, with colorful environments that look like a storybook come to life. The puzzles are clever without being frustrating, the combat is simple but satisfying, and the story — a mouse on a quest to save her uncle from a fire that consumed their kingdom — has genuine emotional weight. Moss is the game you play when you want to remember why VR matters for reasons beyond spectacle.

## #6: The Walking Dead: Saints & Sinners

![The Walking Dead: Saints & Sinners](/images/games/the-walking-dead-saints-and-sinners-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

Skydance Interactive proved a Quest could carry weight, consequence, and a world that doesn't reset when you take the headset off. [The Walking Dead: Saints & Sinners](/games/the-walking-dead-saints-and-sinners) is a full-length survival horror campaign set in a flooded, rotting New Orleans, and it's the most ambitious thing that ever ran on the original Quest hardware.

You scavenge. You craft. You stab walkers through the skull with physics that actually mean something — grab a knife, feel the resistance as it pushes through bone, wrench it free for the next one. The inventory management is physical: you reach into your backpack, grab what you need, and hope you brought enough. The day/night cycle keeps tightening the screws — go out when the light is safe but the loot is thin, or wait for dark and risk everything for better supplies.

The Tower wants order. The Reclaimed want freedom. Neither trusts you, and every decision about who to help and who to betray has weight that carries forward. This isn't a wave shooter with a Walking Dead skin. It's a survival RPG that happens to be in VR, and it proved that standalone hardware could deliver an experience that felt complete, consequential, and worth the full price of admission.

## #7: Tetris Effect

![Tetris Effect](/images/games/tetris-effect-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

[Tetris Effect](/games/tetris-effect) has been on Quest since May 2020, and it remains the most beautiful thing in the library. The synesthetic trip that makes the original so special — blocks fall, zones trigger, the whole world breathes with the music — gains weight when it surrounds you. You're not watching a light show. You're inside one.

The sharper LCD and 90Hz refresh (on Quest 2) turned a pleasant experience into something close to a concert, but the Quest 1 version was already the game's spiritual home. The OLED blacks make the void between pieces feel infinite, and the 72Hz was smooth enough to keep the rhythm flowing without the judder that breaks immersion.

It's the game you play after a session of Saints & Sinners. The palate cleanser. The moment of calm between the horror and the action. Tetris Effect doesn't ask much of you — just stack blocks and let the world dissolve around you — and in return it gives you something that no other game in the library offers: pure, uncomplicated beauty.

## #8: Walkabout Mini Golf

![Walkabout Mini Golf](/images/games/walkabout-mini-golf-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

Mighty Coconut's [Walkabout Mini Golf](/games/walkabout-mini-golf) is the quiet MVP of the Quest 1 library. The physics are impeccable: putts roll true, wind matters, and the impossible courses are gorgeous enough to make you forget you're playing minigolf in your living room. Up to five players can join cross-platform, which makes it the chillest, most-loved social game in the library.

It doesn't shout. It doesn't have a battle pass or a leaderboard obsession. It's just the perfect thing to play with a friend who's never touched VR, because swinging a club feels like nothing else needs explaining. The courses range from serene island layouts to impossible Escher-inspired designs that twist your spatial reasoning into knots. New courses arrived regularly, each one a miniature vacation.

Walkabout Mini Golf is the game that proved VR didn't need to be intense to be essential. Sometimes the best thing you can do in a headset is putt around a gorgeous course with a friend, chat about nothing, and forget you're wearing a computer on your face. That's a rare kind of magic, and Walkabout found it.

## #9: Gorilla Tag

![Gorilla Tag](/images/games/gorilla-tag-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

Another Axiom dropped a free game on App Lab in 2021 and accidentally created a phenomenon. [Gorilla Tag](/games/gorilla-tag) has no guns, no story, no progression system, no battle pass. You're a gorilla. You move by swinging your arms. You play tag. That's it. And somehow it became the most important multiplayer game on the Quest.

The locomotion is the innovation. You grab surfaces with your hands and fling yourself forward, climbing walls, scaling trees, launching off rooftops. It's physically demanding in a way that no other VR game matches — play for twenty minutes and your shoulders are burning. The simplicity of the concept belies the depth of the movement system, which rewards practice and creativity in ways that keep players coming back for months.

Gorilla Tag went from App Lab curiosity to Quest store essential, eventually hitting over a million daily active users. It proved that the best VR experiences don't need big budgets or photorealistic graphics. They need a concept that works in 3D space, a multiplayer loop that keeps people coming back, and a price point of zero. Free, physical, and endlessly replayable — Gorilla Tag is the VR equivalent of a playground.

## #10: The Thrill of the Fight

![The Thrill of the Fight](/images/games/the-thrill-of-the-fight-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

[The Thrill of the Fight](/games/the-thrill-of-the-fight) is the best workout in VR, and it's not close. You step into a boxing ring with an AI opponent, and the only thing between you and a beating is your own体力 and the willingness to throw punches for fifteen minutes straight. There's no gamey abstraction here — no health bars, no special moves, no energy meters. Just you, the other guy, and the physics-based combat that rewards actual boxing technique.

The Quest version is the definitive one. No cable means you can move freely, circle your opponent, duck and weave without worrying about tripping over a wire. The tracking keeps up with fast head movement, and the opponent AI is tuned well enough that each fight feels like a genuine challenge rather than a pattern to memorize. Win or lose, you're drenched in sweat afterward.

It's the game that turned VR from entertainment into exercise equipment. No other Quest title has generated as many "I can't believe that counted as a workout" stories. The Thrill of the Fight doesn't care about your gaming credentials — it cares about whether you can keep your hands up for three rounds. That simplicity, executed with this level of physical fidelity, makes it one of the Quest 1's most lasting contributions to VR.

## #11: I Expect You To Die 2

![I Expect You To Die 2](/images/games/i-expect-you-to-die-2-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

Schell Games' spy puzzle sequel improves on the original in every way that matters. [I Expect You To Die 2](/games/i-expect-you-to-die-2) drops you into elaborate deathtraps — a villain's lair, a speeding train, a tropical island — and tasks you with escaping using only your wits and whatever you can reach. The puzzles are clever, the humor is dry, and the sense of accomplishment when you crack a room without dying is deeply satisfying.

The Quest version runs cleanly, with detailed environments that reward observation. Look closely at a painting and it might hide a code. Pick up a phone and you might overhear a clue. The game rewards curiosity and punishes impatience, which is exactly what a puzzle game should do. The writing is sharp — full of Bond-villain monologues and spy-movie tropes played with affection rather than parody.

Where the first game felt like a clever tech demo, the sequel feels like a complete experience. The puzzles are more complex, the scenarios more varied, and the production values higher. It's the kind of game you play in short bursts — one room per sitting — and think about between sessions. That's the mark of a good puzzle game, and I Expect You To Die 2 nails it.

## #12: Vader Immortal

![Vader Immortal](/images/games/vader-immortal-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

ILMxLAB's [Vader Immortal](/games/vader-immortal) is three episodes of pure Star Wars fantasy, and the Lightsaber Dojo alone justifies the price of admission. You stand in Darth Vader's castle on Mustafar, you ignite a lightsaber, and waves of training droids come at you from every angle. It's the single best "I am a Jedi" simulator in VR — no other experience hands you a humming blade and sells the fantasy so completely.

The story episodes are shorter than you'd expect — about ninety minutes each — but they're packed with cinematic moments that feel pulled from the films. You're a smuggler captured by Vader, forced to navigate his fortress and uncover a secret he wants kept. The production values are Disney-grade: weighty gunplay, tactile droid repair, and a Yoda encounter performed by Frank Oz himself that lands harder in a headset than it ever could on a screen.

Vader Immortal is the game you show someone who thinks VR is just games. It's a Star Wars movie you can walk through. It's proof that the headset can deliver spectacle that rivals the screen. Short? Yes. But those ninety minutes are worth more than most ten-hour games.

## #13: The Room VR: A Dark Matter

![The Room VR: A Dark Matter](/images/games/the-room-vr-a-dark-matter-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

Fireproof Games translated their beloved puzzle franchise into VR with [The Room VR: A Dark Matter](/games/the-room-vr-a-dark-matter), and the result is the most elegant puzzle experience on the Quest. You investigate the disappearance of a British Egyptologist by examining intricate mechanical puzzles — turning gears, aligning lenses, solving mechanisms that feel like they were designed by a clockmaker with a grudge.

The hand-tracking-style interaction (using controllers to physically manipulate objects) makes the puzzles feel tactile in a way the flat games never achieved. You pick up a key, examine it in 3D space, and figure out where it goes. You turn a mechanism and watch the gears lock into place with satisfying precision. The environments are richly detailed — dark offices, dusty tombs, cryptic laboratories — and the atmosphere is pure gothic mystery.

The Quest version is a visual standout, with lighting and textures that push the hardware harder than most games in the library. It's the game you play when you want VR to be contemplative rather than kinetic. No rush, no pressure, just you and a beautiful puzzle box in a dark room. That's a rare thing in VR, and The Room VR delivers it perfectly.

## #14: Doom3Quest

![Doom 3 BFG](/images/games/doom-3-bfg-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Sideloaded — Team Beef</div>

[Doom3Quest](/games/doom-3-bfg) is the crown jewel of Quest sideloading — a full native port of Doom 3 with motion-based weapon controls, roomscale support, and the kind of dark, atmospheric horror that makes you glad you're playing in a well-lit room. Team Beef (Dr. Beef, BaggyG, and Bummser) spent months porting the game to run natively on the Snapdragon 835, and the result is better than it has any right to be.

You hold the flashlight in one hand and the pistol in the other. The flashlight isn't attached to your body — it's a separate object you aim, which means you can light up a dark corridor while keeping your weapon pointed at whatever just growled. That single design decision transforms Doom 3 from a game you play into a horror experience you survive. The Martian corridors feel claustrophobic, the enemies feel close, and the dark feels real.

Doom3Quest proved that sideloading wasn't just for tinkers — it was a legitimate way to extend the Quest's library with experiences that Meta's store wouldn't carry. The port runs at a stable framerate, supports all the DLC, and includes comfort options that the original PC VR mod never had. It's the definitive way to play Doom 3 in VR, and it runs on a headset that fits in your pocket.

## #15: Return to Castle Wolfenstein (RTCWQuest)

![Return to Castle Wolfenstein](/images/games/return-to-castle-wolfenstein-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Sideloaded — Team Beef</div>

[Return to Castle Wolfenstein](/games/return-to-castle-wolfenstein) in VR is the World War II shooter you didn't know you needed. Team Beef's native Quest port brings the 2001 classic to standalone hardware with full 6DOF motion controls, and the result is a shooters that feels decades ahead of its time. You dual-wield weapons, physically reload by grabbing magazines, and clear Nazi bunkers with a visceral intensity that the flat version never achieved.

The game holds up remarkably well. The level design is tight, the pacing is relentless, and the variety — stealth sections, open firefights, supernatural horror encounters — keeps the experience from ever feeling repetitive. The final stretch, where the game pivots from WWII shooter to occult horror, hits harder in VR because you're physically present for the nightmare.

RTCWQuest is the port that proved classic shooters translate beautifully to VR. The fast-paced, run-and-gun gameplay that defined the genre works perfectly when you're physically aiming and moving. Team Beef didn't just port a game — they proved an entire genre was waiting for this hardware.

## #16: The Climb

![The Climb](/images/games/the-climb-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

Crytek's [The Climb](/games/the-climb) is the game that makes your palms sweat. You scale enormous rock faces using only your hands, gripping ledges, chalking your fingers, and trying not to look down. The sense of vertigo is real — genuinely, physically real — in a way that no other Quest game matches.

The Quest version strips away the PC version's graphics but keeps the core experience intact. The climbing physics are excellent: you grab with the triggers, release by opening your hands, and move with a deliberation that makes every ascent feel earned. The environments range from tropical cliffs to frozen mountains, each one beautiful in the Quest's reduced but still impressive visual style.

It's the game that proves VR can create physical sensations that flat screens can't. The fear of heights isn't a gimmick here — it's the entire point. The Climb puts you on a cliff face with no safety net, and your brain responds exactly as it would in real life. That's VR doing what VR does best: making you feel something that the real world can't replicate.

## #17: Until You Fall

![Until You Fall](/images/games/until-you-fall-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

Schell Games' [Until You Fall](/games/until-you-fall) is a neon-brutalist roguelite where combat is about weight and commitment. You block with your off-hand, you dodge with your shoulders, and you chop through synthetic knights to a synthwave pulse that keeps the adrenaline honest. Rounds end. You die. The game pulls you back with just enough loot and progression to make the next run feel earned.

The melee combat is the star. Each enemy has a distinct attack pattern that requires a specific defensive response — block, parry, or dodge — and the weapon variety keeps the encounters fresh across multiple runs. The energy system adds a layer of resource management: you can't just swing wildly, you need to pick your moments and manage your reserves. It's thoughtful combat that rewards skill over button-mashing.

Until You Fall is the game that made roguelites work in VR. The genre's core loop — die, learn, improve, repeat — translates perfectly to a medium where physical skill matters as much as strategy. It's the sword-fighting workout you didn't know you wanted, and on Quest it's the game that kept people swinging long after the campaign ended.

## #18: Half-Life (Lambda1VR)

![Half-Life](/images/games/half-life-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Sideloaded — Team Beef</div>

[Lambda1VR](/games/half-life) brought the entire Half-Life 1 campaign to the Quest as a native port, and it's the sideload that proved an entire generation of PC games could live on a standalone headset. Team Beef's port runs the Xash3D engine natively on the Snapdragon 835, with 6DOF motion controls that let you physically aim weapons, grab objects, and interact with the world in ways the original never imagined.

The Black Mesa Research Facility feels different when you're standing inside it. The headcrab zombies lunge at you from around corners. TheHECU marines shoot at you with targeting systems that track your movement. The crowbar swings from your hand with weight that the mouse click never conveyed. It's still recognizably the 1998 classic — same levels, same enemies, same story — but the VR translation adds a layer of physicality that makes it feel new.

Lambda1VR is the port that opened the floodgates. If Half-Life could run on a Quest, anything could. The success of this port led directly to the explosion of Team Beef ports that followed — Doom, Quake, Wolfenstein, and dozens more. It's the proof of concept that turned a closed ecosystem into an open one, and it's the game that showed what sideloading could really do.

## #19: Creed: Rise to Glory

![Creed: Rise to Glory](/images/games/creed-rise-to-glory-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

[Creed: Rise to Glory](/games/creed-rise-to-glory) is the boxing movie game that actually delivers on the fantasy. You step into Adonis Creed's gloves and train under Rocky Balboa, fighting your way from undercard bouts to championship title shots. The campaign follows the Creed film's narrative, and the boxing physics — built on a stamina system that rewards strategy over button-mashing — give each fight a sense of genuine physical challenge.

The melee combat is the draw. You throw real punches — jabs, crosses, hooks, uppercuts — and the game reads them with enough accuracy that technique matters. The stamina system means you can't just swing wildly; you need to pace yourself, pick your moments, and defend when you're gassed. The training sequences between fights — speed bags, heavy bags, sparring — add depth to the progression and give the campaign structure beyond just "fight the next guy."

Creed is the game that made boxing a genre in VR. Before it, boxing games existed but felt like tech demos. After Creed, there was a standard — a way to make fighting feel physical, strategic, and narratively satisfying. It's the game that proved sports could work in VR when the developer commits to making the physicality feel real.

## #20: GORN

![GORN](/images/games/gorn-vr-hero.jpg)

<div class="rank-meta">Native VR</div>

[GORN](/games/gorn) is the game that throws the rulebook out the window. You step into a gladiatorial arena with a weapon in each hand and a horde of cartoonishly muscular opponents charging at you. There's no strategy here, no stamina system, no careful planning. Just violence — glorious, physics-driven, absurdly satisfying violence.

The combat is pure spectacle. You swing wildly, grab opponents by the head, slam them into the ground, pick up their weapons, and keep swinging until there's nobody left standing. The physics engine lets you grab enemies, throw them, and use the environment in ways that feel genuinely creative. The cartoon art style keeps it from feeling gratuitous — this isn't a gory horror game, it's a Saturday morning cartoon where you're the strongest character in the room.

GORN is the stress-relief game. It's the game you play after a long day when you need to hit something without consequences. The Quest version runs smoothly despite the chaos on screen, and theroomscale tracking keeps up with even the most enthusiastic swings. It's dumb, it's fun, and it's exactly what VR was invented for.

## Honorable Mentions

**Rec Room** — The social playground that proved VR could be a hangout space. Build, play, chat — Rec Room is less a game than a place, and on Quest 1 it was where a generation of VR users made their first friends. See [Rec Room](/games/rec-room).

**Echo VR** — Zero-g multiplayer arena that was essential during the Quest 1's life. The servers are gone now, but for a window in time, Echo VR was the most futuristic multiplayer experience in VR. See [Lone Echo](/games/lone-echo).

**Zenith: The Last City** — The biggest new VR MMO of early 2022. Ramen VR built a world that justified the headset as a social platform, with crafting, combat, and a community that kept players coming back. Quest 1 support ended in September 2023.

**Swarm** — Spider-man meets geometry wars. A 4.83-rated arena shooter that combined grapple-based movement with bullet-hell combat, proving the Quest 1 could handle fast-paced action without compromising on feel.

**QuakeQuest** — Team Beef's native Quake port. The fast-paced, run-and-gun gameplay translates beautifully to VR, and the mod support makes it infinite. See [Quake VR](/games/quake).

**QuestZDoom** — Classic Doom with full mod support. The Quest becomes a portable Doom machine, running thousands of community-made WADs at full speed. See [Doom](/games/doom).

**Contractors** — The military FPS with mod support that made it the closest thing to Call of Duty in VR. The multiplayer scene was strong throughout the Quest 1's life. See [Contractors VR](/games/contractors).

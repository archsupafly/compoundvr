I put on a pair of Meta Ray-Ban Stories and forgot, for a moment, that I was wearing a computer. That is the whole trick, and it is the only one that matters. These are not AR glasses. There is no display, no floating text, no heads-up overlay trying to augment my afternoon. They are camera glasses — a 5MP sensor, open-ear speakers, and a microphone array baked into frames that look like the Ray-Bans my neighbor already wears.

Meta (still Facebook when these shipped) built them with EssilorLuxottica, and they land in three shapes: Wayfarer, Round, and Meteor. They start at $299. At a glance nobody knows you are recording, and that is both the selling point and the problem.

The design is the part Meta got right on the first try. The frames weigh about 49.6 grams in Wayfarer, a hair more in the other styles, and they sit on my face like the real thing because they basically are the real thing with electronics squeezed into the temples. I have worn bulkier "smart" attempts. Snap's Spectacles always looked like a prop from a failed sci-fi pilot, and the difference is embarrassing for everyone else in the category. These look normal. That single decision is why I kept them on instead of stuffing them in a drawer by lunch.

So the wearability question answers itself quickly: yes, they are worth wearing in the literal sense. They are comfortable, they are light, and they do not announce themselves. The harder question is whether the camera on your face is worth the trade-offs, and that is where the story gets honest.

The camera is the reason these exist, and it is the weakest thing about them. The sensor shoots photos at 3024×4032 and video at a square 1184×1184, 30 frames per second. Square video is a strange choice: it frames for Instagram, not for how you actually see the world. The resolution is fine for a pair of glasses and nowhere near fine for a phone. I can grab a photo of my dog mid-zoom without fishing a handset out of my pocket, and the shot is perfectly shareable to the people who already tolerate my dog content. But the moment I compare it to the camera I already carry, the Stories lose. The video is the bigger weakness: low resolution, square crop, and no viewfinder, so I am pointing my face at the world and hoping. There is no way to check focus, no zoom, no manual settings. That trust problem shows up in dumb ways. With no screen to confirm the frame, a stray strand of hair across the lens ruins shots I never knew were ruined until I pulled them into the app. The first-gen hardware puts a 5MP lens in each corner of the frame, which sounds like redundancy and mostly feels like guessing. For hands-free capture of a kid on a skateboard or a concert I could not film otherwise, it does the job. For anything I actually care about getting right, I reached for my phone anyway. The audio is a quieter win, and I mean that literally. Two open-ear speakers sit near the temples and push sound toward your ears without blocking them. On a quiet street they are convenient — I took a call and heard my friend, and I kept my peripheral hearing, which is the entire point of open-ear. Crank the volume and the bass is thin and the sound leaks; anyone next to me on the bus gets part of my podcast. For walking-around audio they are fine. For replacing earbuds they are not, and Meta does not pretend otherwise.

Battery life is the first place these feel like a first-generation product. Meta rates about three hours of mixed use, or up to six hours of audio-only playback, and the charging case tops you up with more charges. Three hours is not a day. If I wore them from breakfast to dinner I was hunting for the case by mid-afternoon. The case itself charges over USB-C in just over an hour, and it pogo-pins the glasses into place — a small thing, but it means I am not fiddling with a cable while wearing sunglasses, which would look absurd. The limitation stands: these are an outing device, not an all-day device, and the people who will be happiest with them are the ones who already take their normal glasses off at night.

The software side is where the privacy tax shows up. The glasses pair with the Facebook View app, and at launch a Facebook account was required to store anything the camera captured. I do not love that a pair of sunglasses demands a social account to keep its own photos, and I found the app itself clunky. You capture on the glasses, then the media lives in Meta's orbit unless you export it. Voice control runs on a "Hey Facebook" wake word, which is a sentence I felt weird saying out loud in public. And then the recording light, which is small. A white LED on the frame lights up when you are recording, and there is a hardware power switch on the left arm, both meant to tell the people around you what is happening. Critics called them "pervert glasses," and the label sticks because the LED is small and easy to miss, and because a cheap modification kit can disable the recording light entirely — 404 Media found a cheap kit that disables it. The LED does not, and cannot, solve the discomfort of being filmed by someone who looks like they are just wearing sunglasses. The thing that makes them normal is the same thing that makes other people uneasy. I am not going to tell you that concern is overblown. It is the honest downside, and it is the one no spec sheet answers for you.

Where that leaves the "worth wearing" question depends entirely on who you are. If you want a hands-free camera for moments a phone would interrupt — a toddler's first steps, a view from a hike, a concert where phones are banned — these are the best-looking way to get one, and the only one that does not make you look like you are wearing a gadget. If you want to replace your earbuds, your camera, or your phone, they will disappoint you on all three counts. The camera is a step up from nothing and a step down from your pocket. The battery will not survive a full day. The app wants a Facebook account and your photos live in its cloud unless you move them.

My take: the first-gen Ray-Ban Stories are worth wearing, but not worth centering your life on. They are a confident proof of concept. Meta nailed the part everyone else fumbled, which is making smart glasses that do not look smart, and they are a clear first draft of a category that will get better. The camera, the battery, and the privacy tension are the three things you are signing up for, and you should walk in knowing all three. Buy them for the moments you cannot film any other way. Skip them if you are waiting for the glasses that finally replace the phone in your hand. Those are not these, and they are not yet anywhere.

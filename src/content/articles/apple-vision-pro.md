Straight talk: the Apple Vision Pro is the most impressive piece of VR hardware I've laid eyes on, and it is not the headset I'd point a single gamer toward. Apple won't even call it a VR headset — it's a "spatial computer," and that dodge is the whole story. At $3,499, it's a bet that you'll pay a fortune to stand at the front of the line and find out where this category is going.

This is Apple's first new product category since the Apple Watch back in 2015. It runs visionOS, built on top of iPadOS frameworks, and at launch it throws controllers out entirely. Your hands, your eyes, and your voice are the only inputs. You look at a button, pinch your fingers, and it fires. A Digital Crown on the side dials your immersion up and down — slide from augmented reality into a fully virtual room and back again. Optic ID scans your iris to sign you in, the way Face ID reads your face. If you wear glasses, Zeiss sells magnetic prescription inserts that snap onto the lenses.

Under the aluminum-and-glass shell sits an M5 chip (Apple's 2025 refresh of the original M2 model) paired with a dedicated R1 co-processor that chews through sensor data in real time. There are 12 cameras, 5 sensors, and 6 microphones packed into the frame, plus a pair of roughly 4-centimeter cooling fans and close to 300 components in total. Power comes from a separate battery pack — 353 grams of it — that connects through a locking cable and lives in your pocket, never on your head.

The screen is the part that makes the rest of the industry look like it's playing catch-up. Two micro-OLED panels push roughly 3,660 by 3,200 pixels per eye — about 23 million pixels across both eyes — at up to 120Hz on the current M5 model. The passthrough, built from that stereo camera array, is the clearest anyone has shipped; reviewers who had worn a dozen headsets kept landing on the same conclusion. The field of view sits around 100 degrees horizontal, right in the mix with the best consumer kits. If you care about crisp text on a virtual monitor or the sharpness of a movie on a virtual cinema screen, this is the bar every other headset is measured against.

Now the part that stings. The headset itself weighs 750 to 800 grams on the current M5 model (the original 2024 unit was 600 to 650 grams) before you count the battery, and the external pack drags another 353 grams on the cable in your pocket. The M5 model ships with Apple's Dual Knit Band and offers extended battery life over the original's roughly two to two and a half hours. Even with that longer battery, this is a long flight or a movie, maybe a focused work block — and then you're tethered to a wall. Apple's own launch-day pitch leaned on short, brilliant demos, and the hardware math quietly tells you why: this is not built for the six-hour gaming session or the all-day desk replacement it occasionally gets positioned as.

Then there's the controller question, and for a VR publication this is the one that matters most. At launch, the Vision Pro has no native controller support at all. None. You navigate with your hands and eyes. For productivity and media that's a statement of intent. For games, it's a hole. The biggest names in streaming — Netflix, YouTube, Spotify — were absent from the native app shelf at launch or slow to arrive, which is a strange look for a $3,499 device that wants to be your living room. Developer adoption came in slower than Apple's track record would suggest, and the native app catalog stayed thin enough that reviewers kept calling the experience impressive and impractical in the same breath.

Gaming is where the caution turns into a warning. The Vision Pro is not a gaming-first device, and pretending otherwise wastes your money. What it does well is passthrough and presence, not libraries. The native game shelf at launch was light, and the people who wanted to actually play had to route around the hardware: third-party apps stream SteamVR from a PC, and that's how titles like Half-Life: Alyx end up running on a headset Apple designed to never touch a controller. Outlets like GameSpot covered the highs and lows directly, and PCVR-focused channels — MartydudeVR, VoodooDE VR, Getting Things Dun — found real wireless PCVR worth in it. PSVR2 controllers even work through an adapter. But every one of those wins starts with "if you already own a gaming PC and know how to tinker." That is not the Vision Pro's pitch. That's the community rescuing it.

The comparison that actually matters is the Meta Quest 3. Same physical space on your face, roughly one-fifth the price: the Quest 3 lands at $499 to $999 against the Vision Pro's $3,499. The Quest 3 has native controllers, a real game library, and a self-contained ecosystem that doesn't ask you to own a Mac or a PC to be useful. The Vision Pro has the better screen, the better passthrough, and the more expensive everything else. You pay the difference for clarity and presence, not for a bigger catalog. If your goal is to play games, the cheaper headset wins the argument before you've opened the box.

Demand at launch tells you where the enthusiasm lived. Pre-orders sold out in 18 minutes, and as many as 200,000 units moved during the two-week pre-order window, against initial shipment estimates of 60,000 to 80,000 units. That's a launch that cleared its first batch fast and then, by multiple reports, cooled — through 2024 Apple trimmed production expectations, and the device had by then settled into a smaller, wealthier, more curious audience than a mass-market headset needs. The coverage followed the same shape: Marques Brownlee, Linus Tech Tips, and the Wall Street Journal's Joanna Stern all put in extensive launch work, and the headline sentiment landed as "tomorrow's ideas, today's tech." Brilliant. Early. Not yet essential.

So who is this for, really? Three people, and you know if you're one of them.

1. The professional who wants virtual monitors without a desk. If your work is text, video, and calls, the display sharpness and passthrough let you drop a wall of screens into any room. That's the strongest use case the hardware actually supports.
2. The media lover who wants a private cinema that looks better than any headset before it. Movies and spatial video on this panel are the best I've seen, full stop.
3. The early adopter with disposable income and a hunger to see where computing goes next. You're not buying a product so much as a ticket.

Who should skip it? Gamers who want a library. Anyone who resents a battery that still wants a wall outlet and an external pack. Anyone who measures value in games played per dollar. The Vision Pro is the finest headset Apple has ever made and the most expensive way to play the smallest selection of VR games.

My verdict: the Vision Pro is the clearest proof that the spatial future is real and the least practical way to live in it today. Buy it for the screen and the presence. Do not buy it expecting to game. At $3,499, it earns the label "impressive technology, questionable practicality" — and that's the most honest sentence you can write about it.

The first time Defector straps you to the open hatch of a private jet at cruising altitude, machine gun in hand, fighter jets peeling in from the clouds — that's the game it wants to be for all five missions. For about twenty minutes, it is.

Defector is a VR-native spy thriller from Twisted Pixel, the studio behind Wilson's Heart, published by Oculus Studios. You're an elite undercover operative hunting the scattered pieces of a doomsday device across five missions: a villain's private jet, a parachute transfer between planes, a market in India, a London safehouse, a Liberty Island finale. The variety keeps the spy fantasy fresh — one mission you're working a crowded market, the next you're breaching a London safehouse — and the branch choices genuinely change how each plays out. Each mission drops an "ACTION BRANCH" on you — a real choice between two wildly different approaches, and those branches converge on two distinct endings. A single pass runs three to five hours. Quickplay drops you straight to the branch points if you want to see the road not taken, and there's a rack of unlockable cheat codes if you'd rather pixelate the whole game or make your gun explode if you reload too slow.

This is the part Defector nails. The production values are real — motion-captured performances, actual voice acting, sets that feel built rather than generated. For a small-studio VR title, the gap between this and a flat-screen B-movie is wider than you'd expect — the capture was genuine, not mocap-lite, and it shows in every conversation. Slipping a Mission Impossible–style rubber mask onto a captured guard and walking past his colleagues, your face now his, is the kind of "I am in a spy movie" beat VR sells better than any flat screen. The shock-powered gauntlet that lets you grab a goon and ragdoll him into a wall is dumb in the best way. The mid-air parachute transfer between planes is the sequence I'd point to if someone asked why this genre works in VR — it's the kind of moment that justifies the headset on its own. When Defector commits to spectacle, it hands you the power fantasy cleanly.

You hold two Touch controllers and they are your hands. Guns reload the honest way: eject the magazine, reach to your belt, grab a fresh one, slot it home. Doors open by pushing them. Nothing here fights you; the game earns its Bond moments by making the mundane gestures feel like part of the fantasy rather than busywork. You lean over consoles and read dossiers on the people in the room so you can leverage their secrets in conversation. That spycraft layer — picking the right line of dialogue, reading a target's file before you talk to them — is the quiet strength the shooting never quite matches. It's not deep, but it makes you feel like an operative rather than a tourist with a gun, and that distinction is most of what separates Defector from a straight shooter.

Here's the thing you need to know before you buy: Defector has no teleport. Movement is smooth locomotion only. That's unusual for a story-driven VR action game of this kind, and it matters. The good news is the comfort toolkit is thorough — snap turning, an FOV-dimming "Tunneling Mode," adjustable turn velocity and walking speed, a strafe toggle, and a camera-shake toggle you'll want to flip off during the violent swing sequences. The store rates it "comfortable," but that label is generous for a smooth-locomotion game. If free movement in VR makes you queasy, this one will test you; the options help, but they don't turn it into a teleport game.

Underneath the set pieces, Defector is a thin arcade shooter. Three enemy types — rifle grunts, grenade tossers, and the occasional Terminator-sized robot — with infinite ammo and the kind of bullet-sponge AI that stands still and waits to die. Weapons differ mostly by fire rate. The dialogue trees, for all the dossier-reading buildup, usually funnel you to one correct answer, with dead-end NPCs that lead nowhere. The five missions play like a highlight reel with the connective tissue left on the cutting-room floor — you never spend long enough with any character to care that the villain turns out to be a double agent.

None of this is a secret the game tries to hide. It launched at twenty bucks, a price that tells you exactly what it thinks it is: a cheap thrill, not a deep one. Next to Blood & Truth on PSVR — the benchmark for cinematic VR shooters — Defector is the leaner entry: lighter on mechanical depth, but its set-piece craft and spy fantasy hold their own.

Defector is a Meta PC store exclusive. There is no Steam version and there never was. If you've got a Rift or Rift S, you buy it there and play. If you've got a Quest, you buy it on the PC store and stream it over Link, Air Link, or Virtual Desktop — that works, and it's how most people will actually run it, though the Quest store itself will flag it as incompatible if you try to buy it natively, because there is no native Quest build. If you're on a Valve Index or Vive, the Revive compatibility layer runs it — near-native on Index thanks to the controller parity, clunkier on Vive wands.

One more thing worth knowing: Twisted Pixel doesn't exist anymore. Meta closed the studio, and Defector is now an orphaned title. It's still for sale, it still runs offline, and it's not losing anything to the shutdown — but it will never get another patch, and it will never come to Quest natively. What you buy is what you get.

It's a lightweight native title and runs comfortably on a mainstream PC; the complaints at launch were about a soft, aliased image more than frame rate, which tracks for a game built on a small studio's custom engine rather than a bleeding-edge one.

If the spy power fantasy is your jam and you've got any PCVR path, Defector is an easy yes — a string of genuinely great "I can't believe I'm doing this" VR moments built around a shallow but competent arcade shooter. The combat is thin, there's no teleport, and there's no native Quest build — those are the trade-offs, not the thesis. For spy-fantasy fans on any PCVR headset, this is a full-price recommendation, not a sale-only gamble. If you need real mechanical depth or a native Quest version, it isn't your game — but the private-jet gunfight is worth the price of admission on its own.

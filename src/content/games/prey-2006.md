I remember the first time a wall became a floor in Prey. In VR, that moment lands harder than it ever did on a monitor — your eyes know the geometry is lying to you, and your stomach files a polite objection. That single trick is why this 2006 cult shooter is worth a second look in a headset.

First, the obvious disambiguation: this is the Human Head Studios game on a modified Doom 3 engine — portals, rotating gravity, and a spirit-walk mode where you leave your body behind. It is not the 2017 Arkane title. Different studio, different everything. If you owned the PC copy and it's been sitting in your library, you already own most of what you need.

## Two ways to put Prey on your headset

There are two VR paths here, and they are not the same experience. The real one is PreyVR, an open-source standalone port from the Team Beef / lvonasek lineage — the same hands behind the Doom 3 and Quake 4 Quest ports. It ships as a sideloaded APK for Meta Quest 2 and Pico 4, built from the engine work behind the Doom 3 and Quake 4 Quest ports. This is a proper 6DOF port: you aim with motion controllers, the rifle sits two-handed, there's a weapon wheel, and a crosshair pulled from the Doom 3 Quest project. You supply the game files from a PC copy you own; the port is the engine wrapper, not the content.

The second path is VorpX on PCVR — stereoscopic injection with head tracking and your gamepad or mouse, no motion controls, no hand presence, no VR UI. It's the fallback for anyone on a PCVR headset who doesn't want the standalone route, and it's a different, lesser thing.

## The geometry is the whole point

The reason to bother is the spatial design. Prey's gimmick is surfaces that aren't where they should be: portals that drop you across the map, gravity panels that flip the floor onto the wall, walkways that curve in ways a flat screen quietly fudges. In the headset those tricks stop being abstract and start being physical. You crane your neck to find the portal exit. You plant your feet wrong because the floor just became a wall. It's the kind of game where the VR translation does actual work instead of just bolting a scope onto a monitor.

The spirit-walk sections — where Tommy slips out of his body as a spectral form and phases through barriers — read completely differently in VR. You're floating, disembodied, scanning a space you can't physically reach. It's a neat change of pace, and the port handles it cleanly.

Controls are straightforward once you're in — two-hand rifle aiming feels right, the weapon wheel keeps your loadout manageable, and the portal and gravity-flip sequences are the kind of spatial dislocation that can turn your stomach if you're sensitive to it.

## What actually works

The portal and gravity mechanics are the headline, and they survive the jump to VR better than most 2006 shooters would. Performance on standalone is efficient — this is a Doom 3-era id Tech 4 game, and Quest and Pico hardware runs it at a smooth framerate without breaking a sweat. Motion-control aiming gives the gunplay actual weight instead of a reticle you nudge, and recent builds add a hidden cheat menu. And the whole thing costs you nothing beyond a sideload and some file copying if you already own the PC copy; you're paying with setup time, not a second purchase.

## Where it shows the seams

This is a community port, and it wears the usual marks. You're sideloading an APK and copying assets — not difficult, but it's tinkerer territory, and save compatibility is finicky enough that the maintainers warn against mixing incompatible saves. On PCVR, the only option is VorpX, which means stereoscopic 3D and head tracking but no motion controls and no VR UI — you're aiming with a gamepad through a flat-projected lens, a step removed from the standalone build. And underneath the cool geometry, Prey is a short, linear 2006 shooter with a thin story. The spectacle carries it, but there isn't a deep world to inhabit once the portal novelty wears off.

That last point is the honest limit. Prey was never a role-playing world you'd live inside — it's a tightly scripted ride with a great visual hook. In VR that hook does more lifting than it did on a screen, but it's still carrying a shooter that runs about as long and as deep as you'd expect from its era.

If you've got a Quest or Pico and a dusty PC copy of Prey, the standalone port is an easy yes — a genuinely fun few hours of spatial weirdness that plays great on standalone hardware, and it's been kept current with steady updates. PCVR-only players should know they're getting the injection-treatment version, which is a different, lesser experience and shouldn't be judged as the same build. It's a B-tier: worth playing, with real caveats, and the caveats are about the platform you're standing on more than the game itself.

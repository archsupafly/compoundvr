I spend most of my VR time in games that want me to shoot something or open a door. Synth Riders wants me to dance, and it does not care whether I'm any good at it. That's the whole pitch, and it's a better pitch than it sounds.

There is no flat-screen version of this game. Synth Riders was built for headsets from the first line of code — Kluge Interactive shipped it as a VR-native rhythm title, and that changes everything about how it feels. You are not playing a monitor game with a headset bolted on. You are standing in a tunnel of light with colored spheres flying at your hands and a synthwave bassline doing the rest of the work.

The release that matters here landed May 21, 2019, on SteamVR and the original Oculus Quest. That's the moment VR players could actually buy it and play it, and that's the version this article lives in. It has since spread to basically every headset that matters — PlayStation VR, the Quest line, PSVR2, Pico, and the full PCVR spread through Steam — but the experience it delivered on day one is the experience it still delivers. There's no mod to install, no injector to configure, no profile to chase. You buy it on the store your headset uses and you play. Setup burden doesn't get lower than that.

How it plays is the part people get wrong when they file it under "Beat Saber but with different songs." It isn't that. Beat Saber is about precision: you slice blocks on a line with the correct saber, on beat, cleanly. Synth Riders is about flow. Notes are spheres you catch with your hands, rails you trace by carrying a controller through a glowing path, and obstacles you lean or step around. Your score rewards riding the rhythm with your whole body, not nailing a perfect wrist angle. If Beat Saber is a fencing match, Synth Riders is a nightclub. There's no artificial locomotion — the notes come to you — so comfort is a non-issue; you stand or use a roomscale space and move only because the music makes you want to, which is the kind of exertion that reads as a workout, not as motion sickness.

The catalog is the first thing that makes it stick. The base game ships with a tracklist, and then the long tail is paid music packs — synthwave, EDM, rock, and name-brand collaborations that pull in people who would never call themselves rhythm-game players. The Linkin Park and Queen packs exist because the game's whole identity is "the song you already love, now something your body does." That's a stronger hook than any original score, and Kluge leaned into it. Stages — the selectable environments — pair with any song, so you're not locked into a one-to-one mapping of track to backdrop. You can run a driving synthwave tunnel under a rock track and it just works.

Then there are the "Experiences," which are bespoke visual pieces synced to specific songs. They're the closest the game gets to a music video you stand inside, and they're the clearest proof that this is a VR-native title thinking about presence, not a port checking boxes.

Multiplayer is the other pillar. Online play throws up to ten players into the same song for a high-score race, and there's a local Party mode that's exactly what it sounds like — you pass the headset between friends and compare humiliation. Rhythm games live or die on the "one more run" loop, and a scoreboard full of names you know is a better reason to queue another song than any single-player ladder.

Where it lands for me is comfort-adjacent in the best way: this is a game I can hand to a friend who's never worn a headset, watch them flail at a song they love, and have them ask for another track instead of asking where the bathroom is. That beginner-friendliness is a real strength — it's the difference between a game that lives on my shelf and one that lives in my living room.

The honest weaknesses are narrower than the rhythm-genre skeptics claim, but they're real. The first is the comparison that will not die: if you came up on Beat Saber and you want that clean, skill-ceiling, competitive-slicing feel, Synth Riders will feel loose. The scoring is forgiving by design, and some players read that forgiveness as a lack of depth. I don't — flow is the point — but I'm not going to pretend the leaderboard grind here has the same razor's-edge mastery loop. It's a different game, and "different" means someone who only wants precision will bounce.

The second is content economics. The base tracklist is fine, but the game's real library is behind paid packs, and the value proposition shifts depending on how much you already like the artists on offer. If none of the pack catalogs speak to you, you're paying full price for a smaller slice of the experience than the store page implies. That's not a flaw in the game so much as a purchasing decision you have to make with your own taste in mind — but it's the one caveat I'd put in front of a buyer before they hit checkout.

The third is hardware-fit drift across the platform spread. The core game is identical everywhere it runs, which is the right call, but the control surface depends on what you're holding. On Quest and PCVR and PlayStation you've got motion controllers doing the catching and tracing; on Apple Vision Pro the build uses hand tracking instead, which is a different feel entirely. None of that is a problem with the game — it's a note that your experience is shaped by the controller in your hand, and the hand-tracking version is its own animal.

So here's where I land. Synth Riders is a genre pillar, not a genre also-ran. It sits beside Beat Saber because the two serve different instincts — one rewards the wrist, the other rewards the body — and a VR library with only one of them is missing half the reason rhythm games work in a headset. The implementation is clean, the comfort is a non-issue, the catalog keeps growing through music packs, and the multiplayer gives the whole thing a reason to stay installed. It earns an A: excellent, with the one honest caveat that the value tracks your taste in the artists on offer, and the precision crowd will always want the other game.

If you want a VR game that gets you moving without making you nauseous, that you can hand to a headset-newbie, and that turns the songs already stuck in your head into something your arms are doing — this is the one. If you want a tight, competitive, skill-ceiling score-slicer, Beat Saber is still right there. Both can be true, and both should be on your shelf.

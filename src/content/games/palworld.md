I threw a Pal Sphere with my own hand and watched it arc into a pack of Cattivas. In Palworld VR, that stupid little motion is the whole point — you're not watching a character toss a ball, you're the one tossing it. For a game whose pitch is "Pokemon with guns," being inside the world instead of orbiting it is the difference between a meme and a moment.

There is no official VR mode for Palworld. Pocketpair never announced one, and there's no studio-backed port on any headset. What exists is a community UEVR profile — praydog's UEVR injector plus a first-person profile built by the community — that converts the third-person survival sandbox into a stereoscopic, head-tracked, motion-controlled experience. The profile to grab drops you into first-person, hides your character's body so you're not staring at a floating torso, retools the aiming so guns sit right in your hands, and keeps mounts visible with you properly seated on them. It's still a work in progress, but it already plays — and plays better than the flat third-person view has any right to suggest.

Getting it running means installing the UEVR injector and importing the profile, then doing a little first-person and body-hide tuning. There are no comfort options on an injector path and it's full smooth locomotion, so it sits at moderate intensity. Once you're in, the controls are the headline: guns aim with your controllers, you raise your hands, line up the shot, and fire. Catching Pals works the way it should in VR — you physically throw the Pal Sphere. On the newer full-hand profiles for current builds, melee is real arm swings, not a button press. It runs on PCVR through SteamVR, and Quest headsets handle it fine streamed from a PC over a local network.

Palworld is a UE5 open-world game, and UEVR renders the entire thing in stereo on top of that — this is Heavy Demand on a PC. A high-end rig keeps dense bases and packed Pal herds at a clean framerate; mid-range hardware will want settings dialed back in busy areas. The bigger friction isn't the GPU, it's the maturity. The profile is explicitly still in development, and the full-hand version carries a fair number of visual bugs by the author's own description — flickering, odd model placement, the usual UEVR rough edges. Expect to re-tune after game updates; profile drift between patches is the real ongoing cost, not the core experience.

## What actually lands

The first-person conversion changes how the world reads. Towering cliffs, the scale of a base you spent an hour laying out, the moment a boss Pal bears down on you — third-person flattens all of that into a camera you're dragging behind a character. First-person makes it a place you're standing in. The catching loop is the best part of the whole thing: winding up and throwing that sphere by hand turns a menu-action into a physical one, and riding a mount you can see yourself sitting on sells the fantasy harder than any flat cutscene.

The loop itself — catch, build, explore, shoot — is the same game you'd play on a monitor, but the physicality keeps you in it. Aiming a rifle with your hands, swinging to knock a Pal dizzy, lobbing a sphere at a fleeing creature: these are small embellishments that add up to a different feel. If you already sank dozens of hours into Palworld flat, this is the version that makes those hours feel embodied rather than observed.

## Where it fights you

It's not clean, and it won't pretend to be. The full-hand profile's visual bugs are real enough that you'll catch models popping or UI elements drifting. The profile is community-maintained with no official safety net, so when the game updates, you wait for the profile to catch up — sometimes a day, sometimes longer. Menus and mounted traversal still lean on gamepad as a fallback, because the flat UI was never built for a headset. And co-op, which is half the appeal of Palworld, needs a steady profile to hold together with friends — don't assume a flawless shared session.

None of that is unusual for a UEVR conversion of a live survival game. It's the tax you pay for playing a game in VR that was never meant to be. The question is whether the first-person view and the motion controls are worth the tax. For Palworld, they mostly are.

If you already own Palworld and you've got a PCVR setup, this is the way to experience it — a janky but genuinely better first-person version of a game you probably already love. If you don't own it and you're not into survival crafting, the rough edges won't win you over. But for the people who fired it up on launch day and caught a hundred Pals, strapping on the headset turns a good flat game into a personal one.

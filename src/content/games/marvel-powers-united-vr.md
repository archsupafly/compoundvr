Put on a headset in 2018, pick Hulk, and charge your fists. The controllers vibrate, the green hands fill your vision, and you swing. The sound design sells every collision — walls crumble, enemies scatter. For a few minutes, you believe you're the strongest there is.

That was Marvel Powers United VR: eighteen playable Marvel heroes, full Oculus Touch motion controls, four-player online co-op, and Thanos at the end of the line. It launched July 26, 2018 as an Oculus Rift exclusive at $39.99 — right in the thick of the Rift's best era, when Touch controllers were still a novelty and every studio was racing to prove VR could do more than tech demos.

The game was the right kind of VR product. First-person, motion-controlled, hero-specific abilities mapped to your actual hands. And for about a year and a half, it worked. Then it was deleted — delisted from the Oculus Store, servers killed on March 1, 2021, and Meta's guidance to owners was to delete the game files "to avoid dead space taken up on your device." Not a single-player patch. Not an offline build. Just: delete it.

## What You Were Holding

Eighteen heroes at launch. Black Panther, Spider-Man, Hulk, Wolverine, Captain America, Doctor Strange, Storm, Deadpool, Captain Marvel, Rocket Raccoon — and Black Bolt and Crystal for the Inhumans fans. Nine villains including Thanos, Dormammu, Ultron, Magneto, and Venom. The roster was the game's best argument for existing, because each hero felt genuinely different in your hands.

Hulk's charge-and-swing was the obvious highlight: grip the controllers, feel the charge-up rumble, and release into an area slam that sent enemies flying. Captain America's shield throw tracked your hand motion and came back. Captain Marvel charged energy blasts by holding the trigger. Doctor Strange cast spells with gesture combinations. The Touch controls were responsive, the animations were punchy, and the full-body rendering — you could see your arms and feet — gave you a physical anchor that most 2018 VR games lacked.

The locomotion was slow, smooth stick movement rather than teleport, with a dedicated comfort mode and hero-specific mobility options like dashes and hover states. It was a deliberate comfort experiment from Oculus, and it mostly worked. You weren't getting sick, but you weren't feeling fast either.

The arena environments were clean — recognizable Marvel locations, decent lighting, enough visual fidelity to sell the fantasy of being a superhero for an afternoon.

## The Wave Problem

And then the game asked you to do the same thing again.

The core loop was arena waves: pick a hero, pick a map, fight waves of enemies, complete objectives, fight a boss. Repeat. The best characters — Hulk, Spider-Man, Captain Marvel — made that loop fun enough to justify the headset. The rest of the roster felt like templates: fire projectiles from a distance, use a special when the meter fills, repeat. Too many heroes blurred together once you got past the initial novelty of their signature moves.

The bigger structural problem was always-online. The game had a solo mode, but it only kicked in when matchmaking couldn't find other players — the system filled empty slots with AI companions. There was no way to choose solo play. There was no offline build. The single-player experience existed only as a fallback of the online infrastructure, and that meant the entire game lived and died by the servers.

The comfort was solid for 2018. Movement was slow enough to avoid sickness for most players, and the hero-specific dashes gave you spatial tools without inducing nausea. It wasn't aggressive enough to scare away comfort-sensitive players, and it wasn't deep enough to satisfy anyone looking for a workout.

Performance was mid-range on Rift-era hardware — smooth enough for the arenas it rendered, though the UE4 build showed its age in textures and lighting compared to what the engine could do by 2019. The game was functional, not beautiful.

## How a Published Game Disappears

The servers shut down on March 1, 2021. The game was delisted from the Oculus Store months earlier, around late 2020. Players received emails advising them to delete the game. No offline patch was released. No refund was offered beyond whatever Oculus's return window allowed.

Because the solo mode was an AI fallback of the online matchmaking system, it died with the servers. There was no way to play even a single mission alone. The files remained on some players' hard drives — useless without a backend to authenticate against.

In August 2024, a group of fans tried to fix that. The Marvel Powers United Revival Project built a level-select tool that bypassed the server checks, making the entire single-player content playable again with AI companions, working progression, saves, and cosmetics. The Discord grew to nearly six thousand members. The organizers said they were close to emulated multiplayer servers.

Meta shut it down in September 2024. The organizers had distributed the game files via torrent links — reasoning that legitimate owners had deleted their copies at Meta's 2021 request — and Meta issued a takedown for copyright infringement. The Discord was deleted. The multiplayer servers were never released. The organizers called it "unintentional infringement" and took the project down.

That's the cycle. The publisher deletes the game. Fans build a preservation tool. The publisher deletes the preservation. The only thing that survives is the argument for why we need something like Stop Killing Games.

## What Remains

Marvel Powers United VR was never going to be S-tier. It was a wave shooter with a great roster and solid motion controls, hampered by repetitive mission design and a structure that wore thin after a few sessions. The best heroes felt fantastic — Hulk's slam, Spider-Man's web-swinging repositioning, Captain Marvel's energy blasts — but the game around them was thin.

What stings isn't that the game was mediocre. It's that it was *good enough*. The VR implementation was competent, the hero powers were fun, the comfort was thoughtful, and the Marvel IP gave it a reason to exist. A flat version never existed — this was built for VR from the ground up, and the motion controls showed it.

Now it's gone. Not on any storefront. Not available for download. Not running on any server. The only way to have experienced Marvel Powers United VR was to have bought it on the Oculus Store between July 2018 and late 2020, and to have kept the files on your hard drive before Meta told you to delete them. Even then, you'd need a community-built tool that Meta already killed once.

If you're wondering whether this is worth playing — it's not, because you can't. If you're wondering whether it deserved better — it did. Not every dead game is a tragedy. This one is, because the game was fine and the death was a choice.

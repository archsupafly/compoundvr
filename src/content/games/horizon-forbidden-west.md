## I booted the PC port expecting a gimmick. It wasn't.

When Horizon Forbidden West landed on PC this spring, the first thing I did was install Luke Ross's R.E.A.L. VR mod and put the headset on before I'd played a single flat minute. I expected the usual injection-driver compromise — a pretty postcard with the seams showing. What I got instead was the opening ride through the Daunt with real depth, the cliffs pulling back in a way my monitor never sold me. It's still a window. But Guerrilla built a world that rewards being stared at, and a stereoscopic window into the Forbidden West is a hell of a thing to stare through.

Here's the honest shape of it: this is not a VR port. There's no official Horizon Forbidden West mode on any headset, and the only way in is a community injection mod for the PC version. If you came here hoping to be Aloy, swing a spear in 6DOF, and feel the machine-gorilla's breath on your face — close the tab. If you came here because you've already sunk sixty hours into this franchise and you want to see it with both eyes, keep reading.

## What Luke Ross's R.E.A.L. mod actually is

Luke Ross has been doing this longer than most of the flat-to-VR scene has existed. His R.E.A.L. framework doesn't rebuild games for VR — it takes the flat render and pushes true alternate-eye stereoscopic 3D (AER) to your headset with a decoupled head-look camera, then hands you the original gamepad controls. For Forbidden West, that means you're still playing the exact same third-person action-RPG Guerrilla shipped, just with a head-tracked 3D view instead of a flat one.

Getting in is moderate, not nightmarish. You need a PC copy of the game (Steam or Epic), the R.E.A.L. mod files from Luke Ross's Patreon (it's a paid tier — this isn't a free GitHub drop), and a SteamVR headset. There's no per-game profile hunting: R.E.A.L. ships per-game support, so you point it at the executable and go. The mod is maintained and currently functional on the PC build — I'd call its support status stable but quiet rather than actively hyped, which is normal for a Patreon-distributed injector.

What you do not get, and this is the part that defines the whole experience: no motion controls, no hand presence, no VR-optimized UI, no comfort options. You're a camera floating behind Aloy's shoulder, looking around with your head, moving her with a stick. The HUD, the menus, the weapon wheel — all flat, all rendered into the 3D space as overlays. That's the injection-driver bargain, and you take it knowingly or you don't take it at all.

## The view is the entire point

I'm not going to pretend the moment-to-moment combat transforms in the headset. You're still locking onto a Tremortusk with a gamepad reticle, still cycling ammo types with a face button, still watching Aloy do her thing from a few feet back. What changes is the scale. The Forbidden West is a landscape game — sweeping ruined cities, redwood canopies, storm-battered coastlines — and stereoscopic depth does something flat screens physically cannot. When I crested the ridge above Plainsong for the first time in VR, the settlement didn't just appear; it receded into distance I could actually feel. That's the one trick this mod has, and it's a good trick.

The performance ask is real, though. Forbidden West on PC is a heavy title even before you split the frame for two eyes. You'll want a high-end rig — think enthusiast-class GPU — to hold 90Hz without reprojection when the screen fills with foliage and machines. Drop the settings and it stays playable, but the visual showcase that justifies the whole exercise is exactly what the frame budget fights. There's tension baked into the experience: turn the world down to keep it smooth, and you've dulled the one reason to be here.

## Where it earns the headset

The campaign is the draw. This is a long, confident Guerrilla story with a cast that carries momentum — Aloy's arc across the Forbidden West, the factions, the slow reveal of what's actually wrong with the world. Sitting inside that narrative in 3D, even as a floating observer, is more absorbing than watching it on a panel. The opening stretch through the Daunt, the first time you crest into a new biome and the horizon opens up — those are the moments the mod was built for. Creators have run full campaign playthroughs in it, including the Burning Shores DLC, so it's not a ten-minute tech demo that buckles at the first open-world load. It holds together for the whole journey.

The machine combat reads better in depth than you'd expect, too. When a Shellsnapper tucks its plates and a Thunderjaw pivots its disc launcher toward you, the spatial read on incoming fire is genuinely clearer than on a flat screen. You're still pressing buttons, but your eyes are doing more of the work the game asks of them.

## Where it fights you

The distance between you and Aloy is the dealbreaker for some people, and it should be. Third-person camera plus head-look means you're a spectator, not a participant. There's no ducking under a claw swipe, no leaning to line up a shot, no physicality at all — your body is holding a gamepad and occasionally turning your head. For a game this kinetic, that gap is louder than it would be in a slower title. You feel it most in tight interiors and climbing sequences, where the fixed camera framing reminds you that the world was authored for a rectangle.

The UI is the other friction. The weapon wheel, the skill tree, the crafting menus — all of it is drawn flat into the 3D space, so you're reading a floating billboard when you pause to swap coils or brew a potion. It's readable, but it breaks the spell every time you open it. And there's no vignette, no snap-turn, no comfort scaffolding: the mod inherits the game's smooth third-person camera, and with no locomotion-to-head decoupling the head-tracked look can tug if you're sensitive to camera-driven motion. I'd put comfort in the moderate band — not a sick-maker, but not gentle either.

## If you've never played Horizon, play it flat

The mod does almost nothing to convert a non-believer. The thing that makes Forbidden West worth playing is the world and the loop, both of which exist identically on a monitor. VR adds a depth of field and a sense of scale, not a new game. So the audience here is narrow and specific — people who already love this franchise and want to revisit the Forbidden West with the headset on, folks who treat stereoscopic 3D as its own kind of vacation, and injection-driver enthusiasts who collect these Luke Ross windows the way others collect native ports. If that's you, this is a clean, stable, genuinely pretty way to spend the campaign again. If it isn't, the flat version is the better use of your time and your GPU.

There's a separate PSVR2 game in this universe — Horizon Call of the Mountain — and it does the thing this mod can't: put you in the world with controllers in your hands. It's a different game, not a substitute. If what you actually want is to be inside Horizon, that's the headset-native answer. The R.E.A.L. mod is the answer to a smaller, stranger question: what does this exact game look like with both eyes open?

## The verdict I landed on

I keep coming back to the window metaphor. Luke Ross's R.E.A.L. VR turns Horizon Forbidden West into one of the most beautiful stereoscopic dioramas you can put on a headset, and for a franchise this gorgeous that's not nothing — it's a real, sustained, fully playable way to see the Forbidden West in depth. But a diorama is what it is. You watch Aloy; you don't become her. For the right player that's enough, and I'd rather have this window than no window at all. For everyone else, the flat campaign is the better experience, and the headset just makes the seams visible.

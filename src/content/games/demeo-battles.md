## You're reaching into a tabletop, not a screen

I've spent enough nights hunched over a flat-screen tactics game to know the gap between "I am commanding an army" and "I am pushing little plastic dudes around a board." Demeo Battles lives in the second one, and that's the entire point. You sit at a physical-scale table — well, you stand or sit in your room with a table floating in front of you — and you reach down, grab a figurine, and move it. The cards you draw sit in your actual palm. The board tilts and rotates because your hands told it to. There is no HUD abstraction between you and the match; the game is the object.

This is the Demeo formula turned hostile. The original was a co-op dungeon crawl you played with friends around a virtual table. Battles takes that same tactile board — the grid, the dice, the miniatures, the hand of cards — and points it at the person next to you, or across the planet. One to four players, 1v1 or 2v2, and the goal is simple: kill both of the enemy team's heroes before they kill yours. Each hero gets two action points a turn, one automatic revive per hero per match, and a closing ring of fire that shrinks the play space like a battle-royale circle about halfway through. Matches run ten to fifteen minutes. It is a board game with a timer on the room.

## It's a download, not a project

Here is the part I wish more VR coverage led with: there is nothing to install, convert, or configure. Demeo Battles shipped as a native VR title on day one — November 2023 — on Quest 2, 3, and Pro, on Steam for PCVR, and on Pico. The Steam build is the same purchase as the flat PC client, so you get both in one SKU. Resolution Games built the VR support in, and it is the only way the game exists on those headsets. There is no mod scene, no framework profile, no injector route, because there is nothing to convert.

It since landed on PlayStation VR2 too, a surprise same-day launch alongside the flat PS5 version over a year after the headset versions went live. Cross-play is real and works between Quest and SteamVR, and private matches run off room codes so you can pull friends into a game without bouncing through menus. There is also same-room local multiplayer for people physically sharing a space, which is the mode I keep coming back to in my head — two headsets, one couch, one table between you.

So the "how do I get this running" question answers itself. Buy it, download it, put the headset on. That is the whole setup burden, which for a VR title is rare.

## The layer that flat tactics never gets

The thing that hooked me is the read-the-opponent layer. In a flat turn-based game you are solving a puzzle. In Demeo Battles, across the table from a friend, you are watching them hesitate over a card, and you know the hesitation means they are holding a board wipe they can't afford to waste yet. That social read is the whole reason this exists as VR and not as another phone-port strategy game. The physical table makes the tells legible. You see the hand move, you see the bluff.

And it is genuinely accessible. If you never played the original Demeo, you can pick this up in a match. Two action points, a small hand of cards, a clear objective. UploadVR called it arguably more accessible and even easier to recommend than Demeo itself, and I get why — the co-op campaign had a learning curve and a dungeon to absorb. This is just the fight, and the fight is the good part. A short match length means a bad draw costs you twelve minutes, not an evening, so the risk of losing never curdles into frustration.

The mixed-reality mode on Quest deserves a callout. You can drop the board onto your actual coffee table, surround it with your real room, and play Battles in the space you already live in. Here it reinforces the core fantasy — this is a board game, and now it is on your table. VoodooDE VR dedicated a whole video to how good it looks in mixed reality.

## What's thin on purpose

I will not oversell the single-player side. There is no campaign. What you get solo is a chain of preset challenge scenarios against AI, and it is, in the words of the people who reviewed it at launch, a bit of a barren solo offering. It is perfectly fine as a place to learn the heroes, but nobody is buying Demeo Battles to play against bots. The game presumes you have people.

The progression system is the other soft spot. At launch you unlock three heroes and three maps, and the rest open through XP and keys — and the unlock menu is tucked away in a confusing corner of the UI, so you may not even realize what you are grinding toward. For a game built around picking your squad, gating the squad behind a slow trickle feels backwards. Ranked mode was greyed out in the menu at launch with no confirmed format, so competitive players were left waiting on a ladder that had not materialized.

And then there is the question every PvP title has to answer eventually: who is still here? A turn-based tactics matchmaker lives or dies on population, and Demeo Battles launched as a paid game in a market where Polyarc was putting a free-to-play VR tactics brawler, Glassbreakers, in front of the same audience. Paid versus free is a real queue-health risk, and nobody had the answer at launch. That is the caveat I would flag hardest to anyone buying in solo.

## The figurines live in your hands

Let me be specific about the controls, because this is where native VR earns its keep. You are not pointing a laser at a grid. You reach out, pick the piece up, and place it where you want. You flip through the cards in your hand by moving your actual hand. You drag the board to reposition it when someone's head is in the way. On Quest, hand tracking works if you'd rather not hold controllers — it is the same system the original Demeo shipped with, and it holds up here. There is even a team-chat button pinned to your wrist, diegetic UI that means you never fumble for a voice-menu key mid-match.

Performance is light. This is a Unity tabletop game rendering a board and a handful of miniatures — it runs clean on a standalone Quest without a PC tethered to it, and on PCVR it is essentially free on the GPU. No reprojection worries, no settings pilgrimage. You turn it on and it plays, and because you never move through the world, comfort stays a non-issue at a stationary table.

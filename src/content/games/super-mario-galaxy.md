I spent a Sunday afternoon standing on a golf-ball-sized planet orbiting a peach-colored nebula, watching a comet arc overhead in real stereoscopic depth, and then I jumped — actually jumped, the way you throw your weight forward and push off — off the edge into nothing and landed on a honeycomb world spinning below me. That's Super Mario Galaxy in first-person VR, and it's the closest I've come to feeling like a Nintendo diorama was built at human scale.

Galaxy was never given a VR mode. Nintendo shipped it for the Wii in 2007 and never looked back. The only way it lives in a headset is through the Dolphin VR fork — the Wii and GameCube emulator with stereoscopic 3D and head tracking bolted on — plus a community camera code by a modder (Mr. Brocoli) that shoves the viewpoint inside Mario's body, so you're not watching a third-person puppet bounce around the cosmos. The current DolphinXR fork is what keeps it running on modern OpenXR runtimes and standalone Quest headsets. None of this is a port you buy. You dump your own Wii disc image, install the fork, wire up the VR and controller mapping, and apply the camera patch. It's a project, not a purchase.

Once you're in, the game itself doesn't change — and that's the entire point. Galaxy's gimmick is micro-gravity platforming across a solar system of tiny spherical worlds, and in first-person that gimmick stops being a camera trick and starts being spatial truth. You look down and your feet are planted on a curved surface; you look up and there's a star field with no floor beneath you. The Wii pointer, which aimed Star Bits at enemies in the flat game, maps to your VR controller's aim — you point, you fire a spray of star bits, and the spin attack comes out as a wrist flick. These are motion controls doing exactly what they were always supposed to do for a platformer: your hands become Mario's hands, and the act of flinging yourself between planets feels physical in a way the original never could.

The world is the real reason to do this. Galaxy is one of the great whimsical space-fantasies in games — every galaxy is a self-contained toy, honeycombs and clockwork and lava-tube slides rendered with that soft, rounded 2007 Nintendo art direction and scored by a live orchestral soundtrack that sounds absurd and perfect through VR headphones. Seeing it with real depth on those spherical stages is a different experience from the flat game's fixed third-person framing. The first-person camera turns "Mario jumps between planets" from a thing you watch into a thing you do, and there's genuine presence here — the kind you don't get from a floating screen.

Two things will decide whether you actually finish it. The first is the build. This is expert-grade: you need a dumped Wii disc (the Switch re-release in the 3D All-Stars collection is a different build and won't help you), a working Dolphin VR or DolphinXR install, VR configuration, and the camera code applied and region-matched to your image. If any link in that chain breaks, you're reading forum posts instead of playing. The second is comfort. First-person on these spherical stages means the horizon rolls when a planet turns under you, and the camera code trades situational awareness for presence — you can't see Mario's body to judge a landing the way third-person players can. Some galaxies are serene floating; others are spinny, gravity-flipping chaos, and that's exactly where the motion sensitivity bites. It's a 2007 Wii game too, so don't expect native-VR crispness — soft edges and low texture resolution come with the emulator territory.

If you can handle the build and the occasional queasy galaxy, this is one of the best reasons to own a Wii emulator and a headset. Galaxy was already a stone-cold classic; first-person VR doesn't erase its age, but it puts you inside the classic instead of in front of it. Skip it if you want a plug-and-play port or if planetary-gravity camera rolls send you reaching for the exit — but for the VR enthusiast who's happy to earn it, this is a weekend well spent.

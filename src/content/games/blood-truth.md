The first time I got behind cover in Blood & Truth, I didn't crouch — I physically leaned. The game didn't ask me to. My body just decided that the wall I was hiding behind was real, and the two guys with pistols on the other side were going to shoot through it if I peeked too early. That's the whole pitch of this game in one stupid, involuntary motion. It's a VR-first PSVR shooter that treats your living room like a film set and hands you the lead role in a London gangster movie.

## What you're actually strapping into

Blood & Truth is a native PSVR exclusive. There is no flat version, no PC build, no port — London Studio built it for the headset from the ground up, and it shows in ways a bolted-on VR mode never does. You're Ryan Marks, ex-Special Forces, dragged back into a family crime war across London. It grew out of The London Heist demo from PlayStation VR Worlds, which was the one bit of that collection everyone pointed to and said "make a whole game out of this." Sony did.

The setup is pure console: a PS4, the PSVR headset, the PlayStation Camera, and two PlayStation Move controllers. No modding, no config files, no PC. You slot the Moves in, the game maps each one to a hand, and you're holding guns. For a VR title this is about as frictionless as the medium gets — you buy it, you install it, you play it. Comfort options follow the standard PSVR cover-shooter model, with a vignette toggle if the leaning and ducking gets to you.

This is a PlayStation-only experience. If you don't own a PSVR setup, there is no other way in. That says nothing about the game's quality. 

## London, but you're holding the gun

The world is the real draw. Blood & Truth commits hard to a British crime-thriller tone — think The Getaway with the ratcheted-up spectacle of a Bond opener. You move through a casino heist, a high-rise shootout, a car chase, a masked ball, and a final standoff that knows exactly what kind of movie it wants to be. The production values are first-party Sony, and the motion-captured performances land with a weight most VR titles never approach.

What surprised me is how much of the game is just *being there*. Walking into the casino in a tux, feeling the scale of the room, watching NPCs react to you — the presence does a lot of the heavy lifting. It's a tightly scripted roller coaster, and the scripting is good. The story gives you a reason to care who you're shooting, which is rarer in VR shooters than it should be.

The campaign runs roughly five to six hours. That's short for a flat game, but for a cinematic VR experience it's about right — the intensity doesn't wear out its welcome, and you finish it in a couple of committed evenings rather than letting it sprawl.

## The gunplay is the point

Here's where Blood & Truth earns its tier. The dual-wield Move controls are the whole game, and they feel great in the ways that matter. You hold a pistol in each hand, you reload by grabbing a fresh mag off your chest and slamming it home, you cock the slide, you aim by actually pointing. Want to fire two guns at once? Do it. Want to keep one hand steady on a rifle while you blind-fire around a corner? The game lets you. There's a tactile logic to every weapon that flat shooters never give you — the gun is an object you manipulate, not a reticle you wiggle.

Lockpicking, climbing, and a few environmental interactions break up the shooting, and they're all hand-driven. Picking a lock means working a tool with motion; climbing a ledge means reaching and pulling. None of it is deep, but it keeps your hands busy and your brain convinced you're doing something real. The haptics on the Move controllers are loud and physical in the best way — every shot has a kick you feel in your palm.

Cover shooting is the core loop, and it works because the cover is real geometry you lean around. Pop out, fire, duck back. There's a timing and a rhythm to it that clicks after the first few rooms. I came away from sessions genuinely sweating, which is the highest compliment I can pay a VR shooter.

## Where it actually falls apart

The combat is thin under the hood. Enemy variety is low — you're fighting the same handful of goons across the whole campaign, and their AI is barely there. They pop out, they shoot, they die. There's no real tactics beyond "shoot them before they shoot you," and the gunplay, while fun, doesn't have the systemic depth of something like a proper VR tactical shooter. If you go in expecting Rainbow Six in VR, you'll be disappointed. This is arcade shooting dressed in blockbuster clothing.

The shooting also gets a little loose at distance. Close and mid-range duels feel great; long corridor firefights where you're plinking distant targets can feel imprecise, partly because the Move controllers aren't the most accurate aiming devices ever made. It's a minor wrinkle, not a dealbreaker, but it's there.

And then there's the length. Five or six hours is the whole thing. There's some replay value in score attack and difficulty, but this is a story you experience once and remember fondly rather than a game you live in. For the right price and the right expectations, that's completely fine. If you were hoping for a 40-hour VR epic, adjust before you buy.

## The score, and who should pull the trigger

Blood & Truth is the best argument for owning a PSVR that first-party Sony ever shipped in this style — a real blockbuster with real production values, not a tech demo with a budget. It isn't deep or long, and the AI won't challenge you. What it is, consistently, is a thrill: you're inside a London crime movie, guns in both hands, and the headset makes that matter more than the thin systems underneath.

If you already have a PSVR and you want the closest thing the platform has to a Mission: Impossible set-piece reel you can step into, this is an easy yes. If you're on another headset entirely, there's no door in — and that's the one frustration worth naming. But for the people this was built for, it's one of the experiences that justifies the hardware. Put the headset on, grab the Moves, and let London happen to you.

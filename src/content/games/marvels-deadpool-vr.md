## Marvel's Deadpool VR on Quest 3 and 3S: what you're buying

Marvel's Deadpool VR is a Meta first-party release from Twisted Pixel Games, and there is no flatscreen edition behind it. It's a Quest 3 and Quest 3S exclusive sold on the Meta Quest Store for $50 at launch, running natively on the hardware. Buy, download, play — there's no setup step beyond the headset, no PC build, no PSVR2 version, and no mod scene, because there's no executable for anyone to mod. The game is a Quest-native app, and that's the entire surface.

This shapes the experience in ways that favor it. You're not watching a PC game get squeezed onto standalone silicon with the usual compromises. You're playing something scoped for Quest's compute budget from the start. The screen stays crowded with enemies, the look slides between a cel-shaded comic book and something closer to photoreal depending on the world you've portaled into, and the whole thing moves like a project designed for this hardware rather than a transplant fighting it.

## Deadpool VR combat: katanas and pistols in your own hands

This is where the game earns its keep. Combat is first-person and fully in your hands — dual katanas, pistols, a grenade, and a grappling gun you fire at pre-set anchor points to yank yourself across gaps or fling lighter enemies around. You draw from body-mounted holsters — katanas behind the back, pistols at the hips. Two independently tracked objects, one per hand, that you can clack together (even if they sometimes pass through each other) or level at a room full of Mojo's recruits.

The grapple is the standout toy. Landing it on a marked point and hauling yourself into a packed arena, or snagging a weak enemy and tossing them into their friends, is the kind of physical gag that only lands in VR. The pistol cycling is where it gets fussy — more on that below — but the core loop of "shoot, slice, yeet" is exactly the power fantasy the license promises, and it's delivered through your arms, not a thumbstick.

## Neil Patrick Harris and the fourth wall

If you only remember one thing, make it the voice work — the best I've heard in a VR game. Neil Patrick Harris plays Deadpool as a motormouth who is constantly aware he's performing, and John Leguizamo's Mojo — the intergalactic TV producer who kidnaps Wade to headline a livestreamed fighting tournament — is a perfect straight man. The script leans hard on '70s, '80s, and early-'90s TV references, which is a slightly odd fit for Quest's younger core audience, but the writing is sharp enough that the bits land even when the references don't.

Structure-wise, Mojo drags you through a series of themed worlds where you fight waves of enemies and capture Marvel villains as "talent" — Mephisto, Lady Deathstrike, Omega Red, and Ultimo all show up as bosses. It's a thin frame, and the narrative is light. But the frame does its job. It gives Deadpool a reason to keep breaking character and talking directly to you, and that's the entire appeal. The comedy is the world, and the world is worth inhabiting for the banter alone.

## The campaign loops before it ends

Here's the honest catch. The encounter design repeats. You portal into a world, clear a wave, clear another wave, fight a boss, move on — and by the back half the rhythm is predictable. Eight hours on normal is enough to reach and beat the final boss, which is a real, generous runtime for a Quest title. But eight hours of the same arena shape means the spectacle has to carry more than it should.

The upgrade track doesn't help. Progression runs on points you spend on weapon skins and cosmetics, with around six pistol variants that mostly trade reload speed, magazine size, and firepower. The unlocks are largely pointless, and there are no alternative game modes outside the campaign at launch. You're not building a Deadpool; you're re-skinning one. For a game this funny and this good-looking, that shallowness stings a little more than it would on a lesser title.

## The holsters grab the wrong gun

The one friction point that bit me is the inventory. Grabbing the grappling gun from behind your back frequently yields the pistol from your hip instead, and the reverse — which can wreck a quick-time event that expected one or the other. Play standing, because trying to cycle pistols and the grapple while seated makes the reach inconsistency worse. It's an interaction-design miss, not a comfort issue, and it's the kind of thing that breaks your flow mid-fight. The objects also clip through each other when you clack them together, which reads as jank rather than the satisfying clank the game clearly wants.

None of it is enough to make the game frustrating. It's the texture of a first-party title that shipped with a brilliant voice and a combat system that needed one more polish pass on the grabbing logic. Comfort-wise, the action is intense and the camera shake is heavy enough to matter — there's a dedicated shake toggle and an optional third-person view for a few forced moving-platform moments, and four difficulty modes plus a "kiddie friendly" dialogue toggle round out the accessibility options.

## Is Marvel's Deadpool VR worth it

Deadpool VR is an easy recommend for anyone with a Quest 3 or 3S and a tolerance for '80s sitcom references. It's a rare Marvel VR game that feels like the character instead of a licensed shell, the motion-controlled melee and grapple are the best thing in it, and eight hours of Harris's delivery is worth the price on voice alone. The combat repetition and the hollow upgrade track are real, but they're caveats on a game that's still worth your time. If you want the best-written, best-acted superhero comedy in VR, this is the one — just don't expect the systems underneath the jokes to reward a second playthrough.

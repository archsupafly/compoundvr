I spent a weekend I can't get back inside Hale House, and the moment that stuck wasn't a jump scare — it was pulling a damp photograph out of the camera, shaking it under a bare bulb, and watching a face resolve where there was only wall a second before. In VR, MADiSON's instant-camera ritual stops being a gimmick and becomes something you do with your hands.

## What MADiSON VR actually is

MADiSON VR is not a mod, an injector profile, or a community rescue job. Bloodious Games shipped a first-party VR adaptation of its 2022 flatscreen horror on the same day in May 2024 for PSVR2 and PCVR (Steam), then brought a standalone Quest build to Quest 2 and Quest 3 later that year. There is no dependency chain to untangle, no loader folder, no "does the framework have a profile" question. You buy it on the store your headset uses and you're in. That matters more than it sounds: this game's reputation rises and falls on atmosphere and interaction, and a native build is the only way you get tracked controllers and real stereo depth instead of a flat screen with head tracking bolted on.

For PSVR2 that means Sense controllers and headset haptics — the game literally buzzes the headset against your skull during Luca's migraine sequences. On PCVR it's any tracked controller through SteamVR or OpenXR. On Quest it's a self-contained standalone build, no PC tether. Same game, three storefronts, one codebase family. On comfort it's well-served: seated or standing, smooth or snap turning with an adjustable angle, dominant-hand and forward-direction swaps, a height offset, and accessibility toggles for head movement, arm sway, and camera shake — and since it's a lightweight Unity build that runs on a standalone Quest 2, performance isn't the story on PCVR or PSVR2 either.

## The camera is the whole game

This is a first-person horror game built around a possessed instant camera. You raise it, snap, and the photo you take becomes a tool. Sometimes it reveals what's hiding in a room, sometimes it nudges the timeline, sometimes it just shows you something you did not want to see. In flat MADiSON this is a button-press with a viewfinder cutscene. In VR it is your hands.

UploadVR's review walked through the loop precisely: bring the camera up from over your shoulder, press to snap, then pull the developed picture out with your other hand and wiggle it until the image forms. Collecting items works the same way — grab, fling over your shoulder into a limited inventory. Doors and drawers need a real grip-and-pull motion on a latch. None of this is motion-control theater bolted onto a gamepad scheme; the VR version was built assuming you have two hands and expect to use them. That's the difference between a horror game that supports VR and one that was rebuilt for it.

The photo-developing beat is the single best argument for this port. Holding a physical print, turning it in the light, watching it resolve — that's presence doing work a flat screen cannot. When the camera is the key to the scare, giving the player the camera in their actual hands is the right call, and Bloodious made it.

## Where it actually terrifies

The dread in MADiSON isn't cheap. It's a slow, claustrophobic build — a P.T.-lineage corridor horror where the house rearranges itself and the demon (Blue Knees, if you've seen the marketing) waits in the dark stretches between puzzles. Reviewers across the VR press landed on the same word: scariest. Gamertag VR called it the scariest game he'd ever played. PSVR Without Parole, The VR GRID, Dave Does VR, half a dozen channels led their coverage with that framing. When the universal hook across independent coverage is fear, the game is doing something right, and it isn't jump-scare spam — it's sustained, palms-sweating tension that the headset magnifies because you are standing inside the hallway, not watching it.

The world is worth being afraid in. The manor feels lived-in and wrong, the sound design is engineered to make you flinch at a floorboard, and the headset haptics during the migraine sequences turn a flat-game inconvenience into a physical jolt you feel through the headset. For roughly a six-to-seven-hour campaign — longer if the obtuse puzzles stall you — it commits to a mood and holds it. It's a full horror game, and one of the more frightening things on the platform.

## Where it fights you

Now the honest part, because this is where the tier lands. MADiSON VR is marred by friction that the flat version didn't have, and the VR adaptation owns it.

Movement is slow — deliberately, oppressively slow, which serves the dread but also means you spend a lot of time creeping. Combined with "janky controls" (UploadVR's phrase, and it's fair) the act of walking the halls can feel like wading through the game's own reluctance to let you move. The contextual grab prompts are the bigger problem. Reaching for a doorknob or a drawer should be instinctive in VR, and too often the game's interact prompt misaligns or lags — the same photograph clue that reads clearly in the flat version sits smaller and slightly off in VR, so you're squinting and re-aiming when you should be unsettled. When the scares depend on you fumbling at exactly the wrong moment, that's arguably good design; when they depend on the game failing to register your hand, it's just friction.

A post-launch patch added a "magnetic" distant-grab option, which helps you snag items without nailing the proximity each time. It's a real fix, and it tells you the developers were listening. But it doesn't rebuild the underlying interaction model, and the puzzles remain genuinely opaque — the kind where you'll stare at a wall of symbols wondering what the game expects, and the VR layer doesn't clarify them the way a cleaner UI might. The original MADiSON was criticized for obscure puzzles; the VR version inherits that flaw and adds hand-tracking imprecision on top.

## The honest verdict

I keep coming back to one comparison the VR press kept making: MADiSON VR sits in the same lineage as Resident Evil 7 VR and Phasmophobia VR — native horror ports that put you inside the thing instead of in front of it. On that axis it earns its spot. The photo mechanic is the best argument for VR horror I've handled this year, and the sustained dread is real, not manufactured. If you handed me a headset and said "scare me for six hours," this is on the short list.

The friction is the answer. Slow movement plus imprecise contextual grabs plus puzzles that withhold their logic means the experience trips over its own hands more than a great VR horror game should. The implementation fights the atmosphere it's trying to build. It's a genuinely frightening game whose controls occasionally remind you you're fighting a machine to be afraid.

This is for the player who wants to be scared more than they want a clean control scheme. If you go in forgiving the grabs and the obscure solutions, Hale House delivers a longer, meaner, more physical fright than almost anything else on PSVR2, PCVR, or Quest. If you bail the moment a horror game makes you work for its own mechanics, this one will test your patience before it earns it. I came out of it rattled, a little annoyed at the interact prompts, and absolutely glad I played it in VR instead of on a monitor.

I've wanted a VR MMO since I first put on a headset and realized every game I loved was a lonely single-player box. Zenith is the first one that actually shipped and let me swing a sword with my own arms.

Zenith: The Last City launched in early access at the end of January 2022 on Quest, SteamVR, and PSVR. It's a native VR MMORPG built in Unity by Ramen VR — there is no flat version, no mod required, you just install it from your platform store and create an account. From the jump it was pitched as an anime-and-Sword-Art-Online-flavored online world, and that's exactly what it is: you make a character, drop into a persistent city, and go run quests with other people who are also wearing headsets. The headline is simple. This is a real MMO you live inside, not a flat game wearing a VR skin.

Combat is the part that sells the headset. You've got three classes at launch — a blade-wielding warrior, a ranged blaster user, and a magic caster — and all of them are motion-controlled. I spent most of my time as the blade class, and the feel is what you'd hope: you raise your controller and the sword raises, you swing and it connects, and heavier enemies want committed overheads rather than lazy flicks. The blaster class turns your off-hand into a charged gun you aim by looking down the barrel. Nothing here is auto-attack-on-target — your body is doing the fighting.

Then there's movement, and this is where Zenith earns its keep. You climb by reaching for ledges, you glide by spreading your arms and leaning, and once you unlock the flight ability you're genuinely soaring over the map with your own wingspan. I lost a good twenty minutes on launch day just flying in circles over the starting city because it felt ridiculous in the best way. For a VR enthusiast who's spent years teleporting around flat-to-VR ports, moving through an MMO world with your actual body is the thing this medium keeps promising and rarely delivers.

The social layer is the other half of the pitch. You see other players as avatars, you party up for dungeons, you trade and emote and stand around the hub showing off gear. The anime aesthetic is consistent and clean — bright colors, big weapons, JRPG quest structure. For the SAO crowd this is the closest thing to the fantasy that's ever been built, and it's not a tech demo — it's a full game with raids, crafting, and a level curve. Ramen VR backed it with a serious funding round and shipped a major map-expanding update, so the world was still visibly growing in those first months.

Here's the catch, and it's the one that keeps Zenith from being an easy recommend. The world is thinner than a flat MMO. Launch-era reviewers were blunt about it: compared to a real PC MMO, Zenith's map is smaller, its quest lines are shorter, and the endgame loop is a grind that leans hard on daily resets and repeatable dungeons. The "hundreds of hours" claim on the store page is technically true if you count repetition, but the content underneath the grind isn't deep. After the novelty of flying and sword-fighting wears off — and it will, somewhere around the mid levels — you're left with a competent but lean MMO that asks you to do similar things over and over to progress.

The multiplayer-only nature cuts both ways too. When the servers are busy and your party is full, the world feels alive. When it thins out, an MMO is just an empty map with a chat box, and Zenith doesn't have a solo campaign to fall back on. That's inherent to the genre, but it's worth saying out loud: this is a game whose quality depends on other people showing up. On the practical side, it runs on standalone Quest as well as PCVR, so the hardware demand is moderate — a mid-range PC handles the SteamVR version without drama, and the Quest build is the same game optimized down. Controls are full motion with the standard VR comfort toggles if the gliding ever gets to you.

So where does that leave you? If you've ever wanted to actually stand in an anime MMO and fight with your hands, Zenith is the only real option on the shelf right now, and the core loop — fly, fight, level, party up — is genuinely fun in a headset. Just walk in knowing the world is smaller than the pitch and the grind is the point, not a bug. VR MMO fans and SAO diehards should grab it; everyone else should try it on the strength of the flight and swordplay and not expect a second World of Warcraft.

There's a specific moment in Espire 2 where you crouch behind a shipping container, peek the corner with your actual head, and watch a guard patrol past in room scale, and for about ten seconds the pitch finally lands. This is Metal Gear Solid in VR. The rest of the game is the fight to keep that feeling alive.

Espire 2: Stealth Operatives is a VR-native first-person stealth shooter from Digital Lode, published by Tripwire Presents, out now on the Meta Quest store. There's no flat edition that needed a community mod, no PC build hiding behind a launcher, no compatibility roulette. It was born in VR. You buy it, you download it, you play it. No sideloading, no dependency chain, no wondering whether your runtime version will cooperate. For a site that spends most of its time explaining how to wrangle six different ways to force a flat game into a headset, that alone is a small relief.

The setup reality is almost too thin to call a section. It's a Quest 2 exclusive at launch and runs standalone on the headset, so the only barrier between you and the campaign is the download bar. The one piece of friction Digital Lode built on purpose is the control scheme, and it earns its keep.

The "Espire frame" is the thing you have to learn. Crouching and crawling aren't a button tap; you tilt the thumbstick and your operative drops into a stealth crawl, peeking over cover by leaning your real head. Climbing is hand-over-hand with the controllers. You pilot remote stealth drones that detach from your body and scout ahead. It throws a lot of verbs at you at once, and the first twenty minutes feel like learning to drive a manual transmission while someone shouts grid coordinates. Then it clicks, and the movement freedom becomes the best part of the game. It offers teleport and smooth locomotion, snap and smooth turning, and supports seated play, so the movement scheme itself won't cost you a session. The learning curve is about muscle memory, not comfort.

When the systems line up, the stealth sandbox is genuinely good VR. Room-scale movement means you're physically leaning, peeking, and repositioning instead of pushing a stick toward a cover node. Climbing a tower hand-over-hand to get a sightline on a guard post is the kind of physical problem-solving flat stealth games fake with an animation. The remote-drone mechanic gives you a second pair of eyes without yanking you out of presence. And the campaign structure, two story campaigns plus a co-op multiplayer campaign, gives you reasons to actually use the toolkit instead of noodling around in a sandbox pretending to be a ninja.

The "Metal Gear Solid in VR" framing isn't empty marketing when the AI is doing its job. The loop is here when you sneak past patrols, time a takedown, trigger a distraction and slip through the gap, and in a headset it reads differently than it ever could on a screen. One reviewer called it some of the most fun they'd had in a stealth game since Metal Gear Solid, VR or no. I get why. The fantasy is real for the stretches where the game commits to it.

Here's the catch, and it's an honest one: the enemy AI is weak. Guards spot you inconsistently, forget you exist after a botched engagement, and the scripting that's supposed to make the stealth feel alive too often reads like it's running on a timer someone forgot to wind. When the AI isn't selling the fantasy, the whole elaborate control scheme starts to feel like a fancy way to walk past sleeping robots. That gap, between what Espire 2 reaches for and what its guards are actually capable of, is the difference between a showcase and a solid pickup.

Launch state is the other asterisk. Digital Lode's predecessor, Espire 1, shipped in 2019 with a notoriously rough launch, especially the outsourced PSVR port, and a long patch tail. Espire 2 is cleaner than that, but it still arrived with bugs and rough edges the studio has been smoothing since. The foundation is solid; the day-one polish isn't where a flagship VR stealth game should sit. On Quest 2 it's a UE4 action game pushing a mobile chipset, and frame pacing is the soft spot. A later Quest 3 enhancement pass tightened the visuals and stability on the newer headset, which tells you the studio is still in the seat rather than bailing after launch.

I should be straight about scope, too. The gunplay is fine, not special. This is a stealth game, and when things go loud it's serviceable rather than thrilling. If you came for the shooting, you picked the wrong operatives.

The co-op campaign is the part I'd actually point a friend at. Running a stealth op with a partner in the same space, trading drone recon and covering angles while you both physically reposition, is the strongest argument for the game's existence. It's the single-player fantasy with someone to share the planning with. Digital Lode has also kept the Quest build alive after launch: an update added a mixed-reality mode that drops the action into your actual room, which is a genuinely fun novelty for a stealth game even if it doesn't change the core verdict.

The one reach complaint I'll allow myself: a PCVR or broader headset release would open this to people who don't own a Quest, and the launch-window hunger for exactly that was loud. But that's about who gets to play, not how good the time in the headset is.

So who is this for? If you've been waiting for a real stealth sandbox in VR, Espire 2 does that better than anything else on the platform right now. Not a rail shooter with a silencer bolted on, but a game where you lean, climb, crawl, and actually plan. Bring patience for the AI and the launch-era rough edges. If you need every system tight and every guard to be a genius, wait for a deeper discount or a few more patches. I'd put a headset on for it, but I'd go in knowing exactly what I'm getting.

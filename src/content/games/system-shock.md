I spent a long weekend locked inside Citadel Station with a wrench in my hand and SHODAN laughing in my ear — and for the first time since the 1994 original, the station felt like a place instead of a map. Nightdive's 2023 remake was already the immersive-sim standard-bearer, but the moment I reached out and physically cranked a wall panel open, I stopped thinking about the HUD and started thinking about the air.

## What you're actually getting

There is no official VR mode shipped with the remake. What you get is a community UEVR profile — a framework injection built on praydog's UEVR that hooks the game's Unreal Engine 4 build and hands you full six-degrees-of-freedom head tracking plus motion controls. Two plugin efforts keep it alive: gwizdek's SystemReShock profile and Ashok0's compatibility plugin, both under active development.

This is not a stereoscopic wrapper that paints the flat game onto a floating screen. You are standing in the corridor, the gun is in your hand, and the lead pipe swings when you swing. A lot of "VR mods" are head-tracking-only — you look around but your hands are still a mouse cursor. This one puts your body in the loop, and the authors are still shipping features (two-handed weapons and deeper world interactions were in active development), so what's live now is already playable and getting better.

## The port that's coming

Separately — and this is the part that makes the wait feel shorter — a full native System Shock VR port was announced, built for room-scale on Quest, PSVR2, and SteamVR by Flat2VR Studios in partnership with Nightdive. It hasn't shipped yet, and the community profile is the only way to play right now. But a licensed studio picking this specific title to rebuild from the ground up tells you everything about how port-worthy the world already is. This article covers the path you can actually put on your headset today.

## Being inside Citadel Station

The thing about immersive sims is that they reward curiosity, and VR turns curiosity into muscle memory. In the flat game you click a terminal; in here you hold a button, bring up an interact cursor, and lean in to read the text on the glass. Hacking a keypad means actually looking at the panel and punching the sequence. It sounds small until you've done it a dozen times and realized you're no longer treating the station as a level, you're treating it like a building you're trapped in.

Combat lands harder than it has any right to. Aiming down sights aligns the projectile with the gun in your hand, so a pistol feels like a pistol. Melee is the standout: swing your right hand forward and the lead pipe, the wrench, or the laser rapier comes down with you. I lost an embarrassing amount of time just bashing crates because the physicality was that satisfying. The motion-gesture melee is the single feature that makes this feel less like a port and more like the game was always supposed to be played standing up.

Room-scale support with automatic height adjustment means you can physically crouch behind cover, and snap-turn is on by default with smooth-turn available through the UEVR overlay. Nothing here made me queasy. The comfort load is moderate, mostly because the game itself is a deliberate, methodical sim rather than a sprint. 

## Where it catches

This is a beta-era framework profile, and it shows. The MFD and main-menu interfaces still lean on chorded controller inputs rather than clean VR panels, so you'll hold a button and steer a cursor to navigate inventories and maps. It works, but it's the one place where the illusion cracks. You're aware you're driving a flat game's menus through VR gloves. The optional PAK that swaps button prompts to match Oculus Touch controllers helps, and there's an Index-controller support file too, but expect to learn the layout once.

## A station that rewards patience

The remake's pacing is the other reason this works in VR. System Shock is not a corridors-and-gunfire shooter — it's a slow, paranoid exploration of a compromised space station, and that rhythm suits a headset perfectly. You move from deck to deck, piecing together what SHODAN did while the station groans around you. The dread builds because you're actually there, craning your neck down a service shaft before you drop in.

Performance sits in the moderate band. Unreal Engine 4 titles through UEVR aren't light, but Citadel Station is a contained, mostly interior space — not an open world choking on draw distance. A mid-range PC holds 90Hz with reasonable settings; push resolution and effects and you'll want more headroom, but I never hit a wall that broke the session. The profile handles the heavy lifting; your job is just to keep the render target sane.

The red-tint remover is worth grabbing if the default palette reads too hot in the headset — a small quality-of-life PAK that keeps the station's lighting from blooming into your eyes during long stretches in the engine decks.

## Who should pull the helmet on

If you already love immersive sims — the Prey and Deus Ex crowd, the people who replay Thief for the ghost runs — this is an easy yes. It takes a genuinely great flat game and gives you a body inside it, which is the whole point of the genre. If you bounced off System Shock on a monitor because the interfaces felt fussy, VR won't magically fix the menu friction, but it reframes the fuss as physical problem-solving instead of spreadsheet management.

For everyone else, wait for the official port. A studio-built room-scale version with terminals you hack with your own hands is the cleaner on-ramp, and it's coming. But "coming" doesn't help you tonight, and the community profile is more than a stopgap — it's a complete, playable Citadel Station right now. I came for the novelty of swinging the wrench in first person and stayed for the quiet terror of walking a dead station alone. The trade is some menu wrestling for one of the best immersive-sim worlds ever built, now something you stand inside instead of watch.

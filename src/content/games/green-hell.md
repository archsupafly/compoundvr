The first time it got its hooks in me wasn't the hunger meter or the incoming storm — it was the parasite. I'd been chopping vines and slapping together a shelter, not paying attention to my own body, and when I finally glanced down at my forearm there was a leech latched on, fat and wrong. In this game you don't open a status menu to clear it. You reach down, pinch it between your fingers, and pull. That single interaction tells you everything about what Incuvo built: a survival sim where your body is the interface, not a HUD.

Green Hell VR is a standalone VR edition of Creepy Jar's Amazon survival game, handled by Polish studio Incuvo rather than bolted onto the flat version as a mode. It's native VR through and through — built for the headset from the first line of code — and it's available on Quest and PC VR at launch, with a PSVR2 version following later. There's no mod to install and no injector to wrangle. You buy it on your store of choice and you're standing in the mud. Setup is about as friendly as VR gets: pair the controllers, flip a comfort toggle if you want one, and go.

What makes it stick is the physicality. Survival games live or die on their loop — gather, craft, eat, repeat — and in VR that loop becomes something you do instead of something you click through. You strip bark from trees with a chopping motion. You notch a bow and then actually draw it, leading your shot at a peccary across a clearing. You build a campfire from sticks you collected, fitting the pieces together at arm's length. None of it is a gimmick bolted on for the trailer — the motion is the mechanic, and the mechanic is the game.

The day-to-day survival work is where that pays off most. You aren't assigning a task to an NPC or queuing a recipe; you're kneeling at a crafting spot and assembling the thing. A bandage means finding the plant, processing it, and wrapping your own arm. A watering hole means carving it out of the ground. The systems are deep enough that you're making real choices about what to carry and what to leave — and because you're physically manipulating the inventory on your body, the weight of those choices lands differently than it does on a flat screen. I came back from a session with sore shoulders and a shelter I was weirdly proud of, which is about the highest compliment I can pay a survival game.

The world is the other half of the pitch. This is the Amazon as a place that's actually alive, not a green backdrop behind a stats screen. Weather rolls in and soaks you. Wildlife reacts and scatters. Your character's sanity bends under isolation and hunger, and you feel that pressure because you're the one standing in the rain watching the light fail. Creepy Jar's flat game earned its reputation on exactly this atmosphere; the VR version trades none of it away and adds the part that was always missing — the conviction that you, specifically, are the one out there.

The signature strength is the body-inspection system. Beyond leeches, you're checking yourself for wounds, poisoning, and parasites by hand, turning what would be a menu readout into a moment of genuine dread. Paired with crafting and archery that actually use your arms, Green Hell VR is the most complete survival sandbox I've worn a headset for. On PC VR specifically you get the closer-to-1:1 open world — the full map and the denser systems — and that's the version I'd point anyone toward first.

Now the caveats, because they decide which copy you actually buy. The Quest edition launched first and it's the streamlined one — smaller, segmented maps and trimmed systems, built to run on a standalone headset. It's a genuinely good portable survival game, but it isn't the same scope as the PC build. Since launch, Incuvo has carried the game to PSVR2 on that same trimmed codebase and added co-op to the Quest and PSVR2 versions, though co-op stayed absent from the PC VR edition for a long stretch, which stung for a game whose appeal is partly surviving the jungle with a friend. The PSVR2 port also took its lumps from press at launch over small areas and load times. Locomotion is smooth with a vignette option if you want it, and the crafting motions are deliberate rather than frantic, so sessions don't wear you down.

Here's the thing: if you've ever wanted a survival game that respects your hands, this is the one. It isn't a tech demo with a hunger bar — it's a real, breathing Amazon you inhabit, and the work of keeping yourself alive there is the most tactile the genre has managed in VR. Get it on PC VR for the full open world, or take the Quest version if you want it on a backpack in the backyard. Either way, Green Hell VR is the survival game VR needed, and the one to beat in the genre.

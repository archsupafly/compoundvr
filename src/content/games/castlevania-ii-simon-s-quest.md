The opening village in Castlevania II: Simon's Quest is one of the most miserable starts in NES history. It gets dark, the villagers lie to you, and within five minutes you're lost in a swamp wondering whether you were supposed to jump left or right. But in 3dSen VR, that village looks almost cute. The houses pop out like little wooden blocks. Simon stands there in the center, stiff as always, while you lean in and look around the corner of a building that was never meant to have a corner.

That lean is the whole pitch.

3dSen VR is a NES emulator that reconstructs 2D sprite games into real-time 3D voxel dioramas. It's not a full remake — it's the original ROM running underneath a machine-vision-style depth interpretation. For Castlevania II, that means the side-scrolling world gets extruded into 3D space: the foreground, the background, the moon, the tombstones, the water. You can move your head around the scene, zoom in, and watch the parallax separate in a way the flat game never allowed. It's the closest you'll get to standing inside a Castlevania game, and it's genuinely endearing for about ten minutes.

Then you remember you're still playing Castlevania II.

The 3dSen VR setup is straightforward by emulator standards: buy it on Steam, sideload your legally obtained ROM, pick the Castlevania II profile from the built-in list, and launch through SteamVR. It supports Quest mixed-reality passthrough if you want the diorama floating in your living room, plus quick save/load and rewind. The emulator even ships a showcase video for this specific game, which tells you the developer actually cares about it. Performance is light — this is NES emulation reconstructed in Unity, not a AAA render — so even a modest PC handles it without complaint.

What it doesn't do is change the game. Simon still walks like he's underwater. The day/night cycle still flips without warning. The hidden mechanics still require a FAQ. Motion controls don't replace the gamepad; you're still pressing jump and attack. 3dSen VR adds depth and presence to the scene, but it can't add what the original game never had. If you already love Simon's Quest for its strange, broken ambition, the diorama makes it feel fresh. If you bounced off it in 1987, this version won't fix the parts that annoyed you.

Comfort is a genuine plus. Because the game is a fixed side-scroller and the camera never swings, motion sickness is basically a non-issue. The world sits in front of you like a toy theater. You can play seated, standing, or pacing around it. For retro games in VR, that's a big deal — too many emulator options strand you in a virtual bedroom staring at a flat TV.

Speaking of which, the fallback options are even less compelling. EmuVR lets you play Castlevania II on a CRT inside a retro bedroom, which is atmospheric and free but still just a 2D screen in a 3D room. Virtual Desktop or Bigscreen does the same thing without the bedroom. They work, and they're comfortable, but they add no VR-specific reason to play this game over a regular emulator on your monitor. The experimental WebXR NES emulators aren't worth the trouble for a single title.

So here's the honest bottom line: Castlevania II in VR is a novelty that happens to be a very good novelty. 3dSen VR's diorama treatment is the only route that gives you something you can't get on a flat screen, and for a game this visually iconic, that's enough to justify a session or two. But it's not a transformed experience, it's not a must-play VR game, and it doesn't make Simon's Quest any less divisive than it always was. If the idea of leaning into a 3D version of a misunderstood NES classic sounds appealing, this is worth the moderate setup. If you just want great VR, play something built for the headset.

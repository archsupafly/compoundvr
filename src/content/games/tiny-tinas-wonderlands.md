The first time I lined up a shot in Tiny Tina's Wonderlands through a VR framework, the thing that got me wasn't the gun — it was the ground. The campaign opens in a bright, hand-painted field of grass and ruins, and when I tilted my head down to check my feet, the world tilted with me in real depth. That's the difference between this and the flat game: you're standing in Tina's tabletop fever dream, not watching it through a window. For a game this colorful and this messy, being inside it changes the whole feel.

## The way in is a mod, not a menu

There is no official VR mode for Wonderlands. Gearbox never shipped one, and the console versions are locked to their flatscreen defaults. The only way into a headset is the community — specifically the UEVR framework, which injects VR into Unreal Engine 4 games and treats this one well, and a vorpX profile that does the lighter stereoscopic-and-head-tracking job if you'd rather go that route.

UEVR is the one you want. It's a free, actively maintained tool, and for Wonderlands it hands you the full package: six degrees of freedom head tracking, stereoscopic 3D, and motion-control aiming. The community has built a dedicated 6DOF profile for the game that you load at launch; once it's in, you attach your weapons to the controllers and you're aiming with your hands, not a stick. vorpX, by contrast, gives you depth and head tracking with your controllers standing in for a gamepad — a Borderlands-VR-style setup, fine, but it's a window, not a room. Both work; only one puts you in it.

Setup is the usual framework dance, not a one-click install. Install UEVR, launch the game, inject, apply the profile, attach your weapons in the overlay. If you've done one UEVR game you know the shape of it. The flat version lives on Steam or the Epic store, and either one works — the DRM that shipped at launch was stripped out before any of this VR tooling existed, so there's no lockout to fight.

## What it actually feels like

This is a first-person looter shooter, so the VR translation is about the gun and the movement, and both land. Aiming with a physical weapon changes the rhythm of Wonderlands entirely — you're slinging spells and bullets from where your hands actually are, and the chaotic, projectile-spraying fights read completely differently when your own body is the camera. Reloading, swapping between your melee and your ranged, checking your ward — it all happens in space, and the comedy of Tina narrating over your shoulder while you physically weave through a skeleton ambush is the kind of silly that only works with a head on your shoulders.

Comfort is the honest catch. This is smooth locomotion only — no teleport, sprint and slide as standard, camera-relative aiming. If you've adapted to smooth movement in other big VR shooters you'll be fine, but it's a real load for anyone sensitive to it, and the action gets busy enough that I wouldn't hand this to a newcomer as a first VR game. The framework doesn't add a comfort vignette of its own; what you get is the game's own movement, nothing cushioning it.

Performance is the other thing to plan for. Wonderlands is a busy, open-ish UE4 game with a lot on screen, and pushing it at headset resolution is demanding — you'll want more than a budget GPU to hold a clean framerate, and most players trim the obvious heavy settings (foliage, volumetric fog, reflections) for headroom. When it's running right, it runs right: the art style is bold and flat-shaded enough that it holds together at VR resolution without looking soft.

## Where it creaks

The friction is in the edges, not the core. The HUD — reticle, health, ammo — projects into 3D space and tracks your aim, which some players find distracting and wish they could pin down. Diving into the inventory, the map, and the skill trees with motion controllers is awkward; it works, but it's the part where you'll reach for a keyboard or a quick menu trick rather than fight the panels in the air. On streaming setups the menus can be fussier still. None of it breaks the game, but it's the gap between "native VR" and "a flat game convincingly draped over your eyes."

Co-op is the other asterisk. The base game supports four-player runs, but its online infrastructure has been shaky since launch, and that carries straight into VR — the reliable, repeatable experience here is solo. If you were hoping to drag three friends into Tina's world headset-to-headset, temper that; the campaign alone is the solid bet.

## Who it's for

If you already like the Borderlands DNA — loot, jokes, loud combat, endless build tinkering — this is one of the better-loved flat games to pull into VR, because the shooting translates and the world is fun to occupy. You need a PC VR setup, you need to be comfortable with smooth locomotion, and you should plan to play alone. The framework that makes it possible is free and current, which means the experience keeps getting cleaner as the tools improve. If all that fits, Wonderlands in VR is a genuinely good time — not a landmark, but a bright, dumb, satisfying one that earns the headset.

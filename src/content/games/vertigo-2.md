I spent a Sunday afternoon in Vertigo 2 with a rifle that reloads by sliding a panel, a pistol that spins open like a revolver, and a shotgun that demands I pump it like I mean it. After an hour I realized I wasn't thinking about the controls at all — my hands just knew. That's the whole game in one sentence: it respects the headset enough to make every gun a small physical idea, and then it gets out of your way.

## A VR Shooter That Knows What It Is

Vertigo 2 is a native VR game through and through. There is no flat version waiting in the wings, no mod, no injector, no "VR mode" bolted onto a monitor game. Zach Tsiakalis-Brown — a one-person studio publishing as Zulubo Productions, with a Valve contracting stint in his background — built this in Unity specifically for the headset, and it shows in the bones. You buy it on Steam for PCVR or on the PlayStation Store for PSVR2, you launch it, and you're inside. There is no setup tax, no community Discord to join, no profile to tweak. That alone puts it ahead of half the catalog.

What it actually is: a single-player, first-person action-adventure shooter with a Half-Life-shaped skeleton. You move through a strange facility and stranger worlds, shooting things, solving the occasional puzzle, and collecting weapons like a magpie with trigger discipline. The campaign runs roughly ten hours if you don't dawdle, which is a genuine commitment in VR terms and a point in its favor — this isn't a two-hour tech demo with delusions of grandeur.

## The Guns Are the Whole Game

Here's where Vertigo 2 separates from the pack. There are fourteen collectible weapons, and every one of them has a reload mechanic designed from scratch for VR hands. Not "press a button to reload." Slide, spin, pump, charge, slap, eject — each gun asks your hands to do something specific and physical, and none of it is busywork. The weapon handling is the best I've felt outside a dedicated gun-sim, and it turns combat into a rhythm instead of a chore.

That design philosophy carries into the shooting itself. Gunfights are kinetic and varied because the tools are varied; you're not firing the same hitscan stick with a reskin. A pistol duel feels different from laying down suppressing fire, and the game is smart enough to build arenas that reward switching your loadout mid-fight. If you've ever been bored by VR shooters where every gun is a laser pointer with a different sound effect, this is the antidote.

## Weird, Funny, and Longer Than You Expect

The campaign's secret weapon is its tone. Vertigo 2 is dorky on purpose — witty, self-aware, happy to be strange. The writing lands because it never takes itself too seriously, and in a medium where so many shooters are grim, grey space marines, a game that's willing to be a little silly is a relief. It's the kind of humor that makes a ten-hour campaign feel like it flew by rather than a grind to survive.

It's also got puzzles, and they're not afterthoughts. The game spaces them through the shooting so you never settle into one mode for too long, and the best ones use the VR space instead of asking you to read a flat-screen clue. There's no crafting and no glowing waypoint telling you where to walk, so you're actually navigating rather than following a breadcrumb trail — a small thing, but it keeps you present in the world instead of on autopilot.

Comfort is handled with the standard toolkit: smooth and teleport locomotion, snap or smooth turning, vignette and seated options. Find your mix and the game won't fight you on movement. 

## The PSVR2 Story

The PCVR version is the definitive way to play — it launched first and it's the version critics put up next to Half-Life: Alyx. Road to VR called it one of PC's greatest VR games since Alyx, and that's not faint praise from a publication that doesn't hand that out. The PSVR2 port arrived later, on January 15, 2024, after slipping from an October 2023 target and then a December 2023 one.

I'll be straight about the PSVR2 launch: it landed rough. Early coverage flagged real problems — render resolution that was fine but could've been sharper, plus assorted image and technical issues that shouldn't have shipped. If you bought it day one on PSVR2, you had a right to be annoyed. The good news is the port got patched, and later revisits from dedicated PSVR2 reviewers are far warmer. It's a real, motion-controlled port on Sense controllers, not a downgrade in spirit — it just needed time to match the PC build. If you're a PSVR2-only player, get it now, not at launch, and you'll be happy.

## Who Should Suit Up

This is a PCVR-first shooter for people who miss the kind of campaign Half-Life: Alyx delivered and want more of it — a real story, real length, and gunplay that treats your hands as part of the fantasy. If you came to VR for flighty arcade shooters or five-minute loops, the ten-hour commitment and puzzle beats might feel slow. But if you want a headset game that respects your time and your wrists, Vertigo 2 is one of the easiest recommendations in native VR right now. The PC build is the one to beat; the PSVR2 version earned its place after a shaky start. Either way, this is the closest thing we've gotten to a new Half-Life in the headset, and it's built by one person who clearly loves the medium. Go play it.

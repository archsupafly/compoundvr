The first time I properly connected with a zombie's skull in Undead Citadel, I felt it in my shoulder the next morning. Not because something was wrong — because the game makes you earn every kill with an actual swing, and after twenty minutes of holding a battering ram of a warhammer overhead, your delts remember the session. This is a game that treats your arms as the weapon system, not as a mouse click with extra steps.

## What Undead Citadel Actually Is

Undead Citadel is a VR-native medieval melee game from Spanish studio Dark Curry — built for the headset from day one, with no flat-screen version to inherit or mod. You're dropped into a crumbling stronghold overrun by the undead and handed a pile of medieval hardware: swords, axes, maces, warhammers, longswords, shields, bows, and eventually your bare hands. The official count sits at over 40 close-combat weapons, each with its own weight, reach, and tempo.

It's a native build on every major VR platform — SteamVR on PC, a standalone Quest version, and a later PSVR2 port — so there's no injection, no framework profile, no community patch holding it together. You buy it on the store for your headset, install it, and play. That's the entire setup story. For a VR enthusiast this is the boring-good kind of release: it just works on the hardware you already own.

The content splits into a single-player campaign that pushes you through set-piece encounters, a horde mode for sustained wave survival, and a multiplayer mode the studio added after launch so you can get swarmed alongside a friend. That's a broader content spread than most physics-melee titles bother with, and it matters for how I rate the thing — more on that below.

## The Combat Is the Whole Pitch

Everything in Undead Citadel lives or dies on how the fighting feels, and here it lands. The melee is physics-driven; swings follow your arm. A horizontal axe swing arcs where your arm arcs; a shield bash stops a charging ghoul because you actually stepped into it; a sword thrust finds the gap your wrist aimed at. Enemies react to where they're struck — limbs, heads, torsos — and the dismemberment is gratuitous enough that "gross" is the word several covering channels reached for first. I'd call it satisfying rather than shocking, but the game clearly wants you to feel the wet work, and it delivers.

The weapon variety does real lifting here. A warhammer doesn't play like a longsword, and a longsword doesn't play like a bow. Switching between a fast shortsword for crowd control and a two-handed brute for the big bastards changes your whole posture in the room. After an hour I'd stopped thinking about controls entirely and was just trying to survive with whatever was in my hand.

Comfort sits at moderate intensity, which is honest for the subgenre: you're leaning, stepping, and winding up constantly, and it's smooth-locomotion movement rather than teleport. Nobody's getting sick from the camera, but your body will know it played.

## Where It Earns Its Keep

The thing that separates Undead Citadel from the pile of "physics melee tech demos" is that it has somewhere to point the combat. The campaign gives you escalating scenarios instead of an endless sandbox with a spawn menu, and the horde mode gives the brawling a score-attack reason to come back. The multiplayer addition is the sleeper — getting rushed by undead while a buddy covers your flank turns a single-player chore into something genuinely social, and it's the kind of mode that keeps a VR title installed long after the campaign's done.

Performance is moderate demand across the board. The PCVR build runs comfortably on a mid-range rig, the Quest port is a real standalone version rather than a streaming compromise, and the PSVR2 edition leans on the Sense controllers for the same motion-driven swing feel. You're not fighting your frame rate to land a hit, which is the baseline a melee game needs and doesn't always get.

And the presentation commits. The gore is the obvious headline, but the audio — crunch, squelch, the rasp of a blade leaving bone — does a lot of the work of making each kill land. When a swing connects and the thing in front of you comes apart, the game sells the violence without leaning on spectacle alone.

## The Shadow It Can't Escape

Undead Citadel released into a subgenre Blade & Sorcery had already claimed, and the comparison is unavoidable because players make it constantly. Blade & Sorcery is the sandbox benchmark — near-limitless modding, NPC variety, and emergent physics play that a tighter campaign-and-horde structure simply doesn't match. Undead Citadel is the better-directed experience; Blade & Sorcery is the deeper toy box. If what you want is infinite systems to break, this isn't going to dethrone your current favorite.

That gap shows up in enemy variety and depth. The undead horde is fun to carve through, but the roster doesn't sprawl, and the AI is competent rather than clever. After the novelty of a clean decapitation wears off — and it lasts a good while — you start noticing that most encounters resolve the same way: swing, block, repeat, escalate. The campaign paces that well, but it's there.

The other asterisk is timing and reach. The PSVR2 port arrived well after the PCVR and Quest launches, so if you're a PlayStation-only headset owner you came to the party later than everyone else. The PSVR2 version is the same game with the same feel; PlayStation owners just got it later.

## Who Should Pick Up the Sword

Undead Citadel is one of the most physically satisfying VR melee games on the market, and the campaign-plus-horde-plus-multiplayer spread gives it more reason to stay installed than the average physics sandbox. It's not going to unseat Blade & Sorcery for players who want infinite systems and modding depth — and I'm not going to pretend the enemy variety is deeper than it is. But the combat feel is the real article: every weapon has weight, every kill is earned with your body, and the structured modes mean you're never just staring at a spawn menu wondering what to do.

If you came to VR for the sword fighting and you want a game that points that fighting somewhere, this is an easy recommend. If you already live in Blade & Sorcery's sandbox and rarely leave, you'll find this tighter and shorter — but you'll still feel every swing. Grab it on whatever headset you own; they're all the same game, and all of them will make your shoulders tired in the best way.

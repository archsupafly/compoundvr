I spent the better part of a Sunday with Wukong in a headset, and the moment that hooked me wasn't a boss fight or a set piece. It was standing still on a cliff path, turning my head, and realizing the layered mist over the valley below actually had depth. Flat screens flatten this game's biggest strength — its art direction — into wallpaper. In VR, the world finally has volume.

There's no official VR mode here. What we've got is praydog's UEVR injector plus a handful of community profiles, and it works far better than a framework conversion has any right to. From the launch window onward, people have been running the game in stereoscopic 3D with full 6DOF head tracking, and the implementation is stable enough that I'd call it a real way to play, not a tech demo you suffer through.

What you're getting is the full game — third-person action RPG, all of it — rendered natively in stereo with head tracking bolted on. The character stays third-person, driven by your gamepad, while your head becomes a free camera you can swing around the scene. A few community profiles, including a 6DOF option from Headset-VR, even let you push the view closer to first-person so you can feel the scale of a yaoguai up close. You play with a controller. There are no motion controls and no hand presence — this was built as a fast soulslike with a pad, and the UEVR path respects that rather than faking it.

You'll be seated or standing with a gamepad; the third-person view is easy on the stomach, though the faster boss fights whip the camera around enough that motion-sensitive players should brace.

Performance is where the fantasy meets the wall. This is one of the most demanding UE5 titles you can throw at a VR headset. Nanite-dense environments, heavy foliage, and the game's baked-in lighting mean you need serious GPU headroom to hold a comfortable framerate. The community's hard-won settings wisdom is consistent: borderless windowed mode, motion blur off, VSync off, ray tracing off, and DLSS doing the heavy lifting with a modest resolution scale. Even then, this is a high-end rig experience. A mid-range PC will run it, but you'll be making compromises that chip away at the very thing that makes it worth doing — the look.

And the look is the entire argument. Black Myth's world-building is the reason to put the headset on. The mythological Chinese landscapes, the creature design, the way light falls through a forest or pools in a cave — all of it gains something real in 3D that a monitor can't deliver. I caught details I'd blown past on a flat playthrough: the texture of a stone guardian's carved armor, the depth of a crowd of lesser demons circling before a fight, the actual distance of a boss across an arena. That's the transformation UEVR buys you here. It doesn't give you sword-in-hand physicality, but it gives you a world with weight.

Where it falls short is exactly where every gamepad third-person UEVR conversion falls short. You're a spectator with a controller, not a participant with your body. The combat reads beautifully — the dodge timing, the stance swaps, the rhythm of a hard fight — but your hands aren't in it. The flat UI, including menus and cutscene depth, occasionally needs profile-level presets to sit right, and you'll be reaching for a keyboard or squinting at text that was never meant for a headset. None of that breaks the experience, but it keeps it from feeling like a native VR game rather than a gorgeous flat game you happen to be standing inside.

The honest fit is narrow but real. If you already love this game's world and you've got the GPU to feed it, the UEVR profile turns a great-looking flat game into a genuinely better-looking VR one. If you're hunting for motion-controlled melee or a reason VR changes how Wukong plays, this isn't that — the pad stays in your hands and the camera stays behind the character. And because it's PCVR-only through UEVR, the audience is naturally the folks who already run a VR-ready desktop.

I keep coming back to that cliff-path moment. For a UEVR conversion of a 2024 AAA, the fact that the strongest memory is about presence and not about fighting the software tells you most of what you need to know. It's a B-tier experience in the absolute sense: not essential, not broken, but a worthwhile way to inhabit one of the year's most beautiful worlds — provided your hardware can keep up with its appetite.

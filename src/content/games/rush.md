The first time you tip your shoulders forward and the world drops away, RUSH quits being a game and becomes a fall. You are not watching a wingsuit pilot from a safe cutscene. You are the pilot — chin pinned to the dirt, arms locked to your sides, watching a ridge rise up to meet you at a speed that makes your stomach genuinely believe you are about to die. Then you bank left, thread the gap, and laugh like an idiot.

That feeling is the entire product. RUSH is a first-person wingsuit racer built by The Binary Mill, the Australian crew behind Gun Club VR and Mini Motor Racing X. There is no flat version sitting underneath it. This was conceived for the headset from the first line of code, and it reads in the camera: you sit inside the suit, not on top of a third-person avatar somebody bolted a VR view onto. You pick a mountain, pick a way to play, and you fly.

Getting into it is about as close to zero friction as VR gets. It's a native title on every store it ships on — PCVR, the original PSVR, and the Quest standalone. Buy it, launch it from the headset, and you're falling within a minute. No dependencies, no config files, no "did I bind the right axis" panic. For once the barrier between you and the experience is just your own nerve.

## Flying with your spine

Steering is your body. You lean — head and shoulders — to bank, dip to dive, pull up to flare. There are no sticks to wrestle and no artificial deadzone to fight; the suit reads where you're pointing your face and your weight and goes there. Within ten minutes I'd stopped thinking about controls entirely and started thinking about the next gap, the next ring, the next near-miss I could shave closer. That's the highest compliment I can pay a VR flight game: the interface evaporates and the mountain takes over.

The speed is the point, and RUSH delivers it honestly. Not "fast for a VR game" fast — actually fast, the kind where your peripheral vision strobes with terrain and your brain short-circuits the part that knows you're on a carpet. Real-time weather and a shifting time of day mean the same ridge looks different every run, and with five mountains and well over a hundred flight paths, the game gives you reasons to return that aren't just leaderboard compulsion. Race mode puts you against ghost times. Time Attack strips it to you versus the clock. Score Challenge rewards the reckless lines. Freeflight just lets you fall and look.

Then there's the sky with other people. Multiplayer drops up to a dozen wingsuits into the same airspace, and nothing sells the velocity like watching another set of suits peel off your wingtip and dive for the same checkpoint you're both convinced you'll reach first. It's chaos in the best way — a flock of idiots in nylon arguing with gravity.

## What it does better than anything else

Here's the uncomfortable truth for the rest of the genre: RUSH is the standout wingsuit game in VR because it has no real competition. That's not damning with faint praise — it's a statement about how few developers have even attempted the pure fall, and how completely this one nailed the feeling. When a niche has one game and that game is this good at its one job, best in class isn't a stretch, it's just arithmetic.

The physicality is the other pillar. This is a game your body is doing, not a game your thumbs are pressing. You commit to a dive with your shoulders. You correct a drift with a tilt. The presence isn't decorative — it's the mechanic. That's the difference between a VR game and a game viewed through a headset, and RUSH never confuses the two.

## Where it thins out

Be clear about what you're buying. RUSH is an arcade thrill, not a simulation, and it has no ambition to be anything deeper. There's no career, no story, no world with characters or stakes beyond the next checkpoint. If you want systems — tuning, progression, a reason the mountain exists — you won't find them here. You fall, you race, you chase a number, you do it again, and it is intense by design: high-speed dives past canyon walls produce strong vection, so walk in clear-eyed if forward-motion comfort ever gives you trouble. That intensity is the genre, not a flaw in the port.

And while the competition is thin, the single-player AI racers and the score chase won't occupy you for months the way a deep sim might. This is a short-attention, high-adrenaline loop. It respects your time by not pretending to be longer than it is.

## The verdict from the cliff edge

RUSH is for the VR owner who bought the headset for exactly one promise: the feeling of doing something impossible in their living room. If your favorite VR memories are the ones where your brain briefly forgot it was safe, this belongs on your drive. It's not deep, and it's not gentle, but there is no better wingsuit in VR because there is effectively no other one worth mentioning — and even if there were, this one would still be the one to beat. Strap in, lean forward, and let the mountain come.

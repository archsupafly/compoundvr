I spent a Sunday afternoon leaning, ducking, and spinning in my living room like I was dodging rain, and for the first twenty minutes I felt like a kid who'd just realized his action figures were alive.

Then the patterns started repeating.

That's YUKI in one breath: a genuinely novel VR idea — your body is the controller — wrapped around a bullet-hell roguelite that's thinner than its premise earns.

**A body-shaped controller**

YUKI is a VR-native bullet-hell roguelite from ARVORE, a São Paulo studio that built this for the headset from the first line of code. There was never a flat version. You play a space ranger rendered as a little action figure hovering in front of you, and you guide it through anime-styled worlds by moving your actual body. No thumbstick. No teleport. You lean left, the ranger leans left. You duck, it ducks under the laser wall. You strafe, it strafes. The headset tracks your position in a 2m x 2m space and maps your movement onto the character.

It launched July 2021 on both SteamVR and the Meta Quest store as a native standalone build, with a PSVR version shipping later that year.

Three modes shape the time you spend in it. Patrol is the story campaign — fly through worlds, grab power-ups, watch the bullet density climb. Endless is wave survival with a global leaderboard, where the score-chasers live. MRcade is the mixed-reality mode: enemies spawn into your actual room, and you're not just dodging — you're throwing punches and uppercuts at the things that close in. That last one is the most physically ridiculous and the most fun I had.

**Dodging with your spine**

The first session is pure discovery. You realize you're not holding a controller to move — you're holding your body. Dodging a bullet curtain by physically shifting your weight feels like nothing else in VR because nothing else asks your whole torso to do the dodging. The ranger auto-fires, so your only job is survival through movement. That's elegant: it strips the genre down to its essence, which is don't get hit.

A run opens simply. You drift through a calm stretch, grab a floating power-up — a spread shot, a shield, a damage boost — and then the screen fills. The trick is that "filling" isn't random noise; the bullets trace patterns you learn to read with your feet, stepping into the gaps rather than memorizing them on a HUD. Patrol ramps these patterns across its levels; Endless just keeps tightening the screw until you miss a gap and the run ends. The leaderboard is the only thing waiting on the other side, and for the score-minded that's enough.

There's no artificial locomotion, so the nausea that wrecks so many VR shooters stays away — what you feel instead is your calves complaining after a 40-minute run. It's a standing game, and you'll want the room.

Performance is a non-issue. The art is stylized anime — bold colors, clean shapes, not a polygon wasted — so it runs on modest hardware (think GTX 970-class). On Quest it's a native standalone build with no PC tether, and it holds up. On PCVR you get the sharper visuals and the widest headset support. On PSVR you trade some clarity for the same loop on a last-gen headset.

**What actually lands**

The body-as-controller scheme is the whole pitch, and it mostly delivers. Full-body dodging turns a genre that's usually about twitch reactions on a flat screen into something spatial and physical. When a wall of bullets sweeps in and you step aside to let it pass, that's a VR-native moment — you couldn't get it on a monitor if you tried. MRcade pushes it further by making you punch, and dropping enemies into your real room sells the fantasy harder than the abstract Patrol stages ever do. There's a genuine thrill in uppercutting a drifting drone that's floating next to your actual bookshelf.

It's also immediately accessible. Buy it, stand up, play. No mods, no config, no dependency hell. For a VR enthusiast who wants a quick physical warm-up or a party piece, that low friction is worth real money.

**Where it thins out**

Here's where the shallowness shows. Bullet-hell roguelites live or die on pattern variety, and YUKI's loops wear thin faster than its roughly seven-hour median playtime implies. After the novelty of dodging with your body fades, you're doing the same dances against the same shapes. The "roguelite" label hints at build variety and run-to-run surprises; what's here is closer to arcade escalation than a system you're actually mastering.

The world-building is anime wallpaper. Patrol has a story frame, but it's a thin excuse to move you between arenas. There's no world worth inhabiting when you're not actively dodging — you're in a bullet gallery with a mascot. For a VR experience that matters more than it sounds: the best VR makes you want to live inside it, and YUKI is a game you want to visit for an intense hour, not inhabit.

On PSVR specifically, the lower headset resolution makes dense bullet patterns harder to read — the genre lives on clearly telling a kill-bullet from a graze, and PSVR1's softer image works against that. The PS Move tracking also lacks the precision of modern controllers, a minor drag in MRcade's punching.

YUKI is a B-tier VR experience: a real, novel idea executed with polish but not depth. If you want a physically active arcade romp you can stand up and play for an hour without setup friction, it's an easy recommend — especially on Quest or PCVR, where the visuals and tracking are cleanest. If you're hunting for a world to live in or a roguelite with systems to master, the repetition will find you faster than the bullets do.

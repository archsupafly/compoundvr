## The bike, the drones, the '80s

I climbed onto a motorcycle in first person, twisted the throttle, and immediately ate a missile from a corporate kill-drone shaped like it rolled out of an '80s anime pilot. That's RUNNER in one frame: fast, loud, stupidly stylish, and absolutely not interested in going easy on you. Truant Pixel built this thing for VR from the ground up, and it shows in the way the camera sits low over the handlebars and the whole world whips past like you're inside a forgotten Akira storyboard.

## Built for VR, not bolted onto it

RUNNER isn't a flat game with a VR toggle. There is no flat version. It launched as a day-one PSVR2 title and showed up on Quest 2 and Quest Pro standalone within weeks, and both are the real product, not a compatibility hack. You buy it on the store, put the headset on, and you're riding. No mods, no injectors, no "well it technically renders in 3D if you squint" — this is what the developers intended.

On PSVR2 the Sense controllers are mandatory, and that's the right call. You're not holding a gamepad pretending to steer; you've got freeform 360-degree shooting and full lean-and-maneuver control of the bike. The headset itself even vibrates with the action, which sounds gimmicky until a near-miss rattles your skull and you realize your body just flinched. A Steam page teased a PCVR build at launch as "coming soon," but the headset-native versions are what actually shipped, and they're the ones worth talking about.

## Riding the line between chaos and control

Here's the thing: at first it feels like the game is actively trying to kill you. You're pinned to a bike at speed — comfort sits at intense, and if first-person vehicle combat usually finds your stomach, this one will test it — while waves of autonomous drones swarm from every angle and the only way to survive is to shoot in one direction while steering in another. The motion controls make that split attention feel physical: you're craning your torso to tag a target behind you, leaning the bike through a gap, thumbing off shots at something in your periphery. When it clicks, it's the closest I've felt to being inside a manic anime action sequence.

## The anime playground is the whole point

What RUNNER gets right is presentation and pacing. The late-'80s prestige-anime look — the saturated neon, the Macross-style mech silhouettes, the synth that hits like a truck — isn't decoration, it's the reason to play. Seven sectors of drone-swarmed highway give you enough variety in enemy composition and set-piece beats that the early game stays fresh, and the soundtrack does real work carrying the energy when the screen fills with explosions. A standout Quest update (the Checkpoint Update) and a steady PSVR2 patch cadence added an enemy and boss intel section and tuned the balance, so the version you're buying now is friendlier than launch-day RUNNER without losing its teeth.

The physicality carries it. This is the kind of VR where your body is actually doing something — leaning, twisting, tracking targets at the edge of your vision while the bike screams underneath you. That's the part you can't get on a monitor, and it's the part that makes the flat version (if one ever lands) irrelevant to anyone who's ridden it in the headset.

## The wall you hit at hour one

Look, I'm not going to lie: the learning curve is a cliff, not a slope. At launch the difficulty was brutal enough that even players who loved the game admitted it took real persistence before the controls stopped fighting them. The patches softened it, but this is still a game where the first few runs will feel like the drones are reading your mind and you're reading nothing. If you bounce off hard games fast, the opening hour will frustrate you.

And the loop is thin by design. This is an arcade shooter, not a campaign with a world to inhabit. You ride, you shoot, you chase a high score across seven sectors, and the levels can run long enough that the repetition starts to show — one reviewer flat-out tired of the extended stages and questioned the replay pull that defines a great arcade title. There's no story dressing you up, no characters to care about. It's spectacle and scorechasing, full stop.

## Who RUNNER rewards

RUNNER sits at a B for me, and I'm comfortable with that. It's a genuinely fun, spectacular arcade ride that VR transforms into something you can't get on a screen — the motion-controlled shooting-from-a-speeding-bike fantasy is real and it's exhilarating once the muscle memory lands. But it's also a thin-loop, high-difficulty scorechaser with no world to live in, and that caps how far it climbs. The reception backs the read: the players who stuck with it call it a hidden gem and a highlight of the PSVR2 launch lineup, while the critics who wanted more depth or replayability came away warm-but-reserved.

If you've got VR legs, a taste for anime excess, and the patience to fail a dozen times before the controls become instinct, RUNNER is a recommended buy at its price — a loud, stylish afternoon that earns its cult status. If you need a game to hold your hand, or you want a world to inhabit rather than a score to chase, this bike leaves without you.

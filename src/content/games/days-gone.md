I booted Days Gone on PC the day it landed, pointed VorpX at it, and spent my first hour in the headset watching Deacon St. John carve through post-apocalyptic Oregon like he was a detailed action figure someone had parked on my coffee table. That's the whole deal with this one: you're not Deacon. You're the guy holding the camera that follows him around.

## What you're actually getting

Days Gone has exactly one way into a headset, and it's VorpX. There's no official mode, no community VR mod, no native port. VorpX is an injection driver — it grabs the game's render pipeline and pushes a stereoscopic 3D image to your headset with head tracking bolted on. What you get is depth and the ability to look around. What you don't get is the part that makes VR feel like VR: no motion controls, no hands, no VR interface, and, crucially for this game, no first-person view. Days Gone is third-person, so the camera sits behind Deacon's shoulder and the headset just lets you lean into that fixed vantage.

The 3D itself is Z-Normal, not Geometry 3D. That's the distinction most people miss. Z-Normal builds depth from the game's existing depth buffer, which makes it fast and stable but approximate — close objects can show edge ghosting, and the sense of volume isn't as clean as a true geometry reconstruction. It looks good. It is not the same as standing inside the world.

## How it plays

You play with a gamepad or mouse and keyboard. VorpX handles head tracking, so when Deacon's bike crests a ridge you can tilt your head to watch the treeline. The community's screen-wrap profile curves the image around you instead of pinning it to a flat rectangle, which helps sell the scale of the open world. Because the camera is game-controlled, fast fights with a horde on your tail can twist the stomach if you're sensitive — you can't decouple your view from the camera's whips and pans.

The scale is the thing that works. Days Gone's Oregon is big, brown, and quietly beautiful — pine forests, abandoned highway, small towns eaten by weeds. In the headset, riding the bike down a mountain pass with the world curving away on both sides is the moment the injection driver earns its keep. The horde set-pieces — hundreds of Freakers pouring out of a cave — have real weight when they're coming at a camera you're inside rather than a screen you're watching.

## What's genuinely good here

The world. Bend Studio built a believable, lived-in version of the Pacific Northwest after the end, and VorpX's depth treatment makes the geography readable in a way a flat monitor doesn't. You see the layers — foreground scrub, mid-distance ridge, far haze — and it gives the riding and scavenging a tactile sense of place. For an open-world game where a huge chunk of the fun is simply moving through the map, that's a genuine upgrade over the desk.

Performance is also reasonable for what it is. Days Gone is a heavy open-world title and VorpX adds stereoscopic overhead on top, but it runs — a high-end PC pushes it at headset resolution without choking, and Z-Normal is light enough that mid-range hardware can find a stable frame rate once settings are trimmed. The stereoscopic cost here is far gentler than a Geometry 3D profile would demand.

## Where it falls apart

You are not in this game. That sounds obvious for a third-person title, but it's worth saying plainly: VorpX turns Days Gone into a very good 3D diorama, not a presence experience. You're watching Deacon. The emotional distance that creates is real — when the story lands its quieter beats, you're observing them through a window rather than living them.

The Z-Normal depth has limits. Distant objects and fine geometry can shimmer, and anything hugging the camera edge can ghost. It's not broken, but it's a step down from what a Geometry 3D profile would deliver — and Days Gone doesn't have one.

And then there's the control truth: no motion controls means no drawing a bow, no swinging a bat, no feeling the bike under your hands. Every interaction is a button press on a controller. For a game built around tactile survival — fixing your bike, crafting suppressors, meleeing through a swarm — that abstraction sits between you and the fantasy.

## Should you bother?

If you already love Days Gone, or you're the kind of VR owner who treats VorpX like a sampler plate for flat games you missed, this is an easy yes. The world is big enough and the riding is smooth enough that the diorama effect fades once you're cruising. If you're hunting for a VR zombie game where you're the one swinging the bat, this isn't it — play the flat version. Days Gone in VR is a great way to revisit a game you liked, not a reason to put the headset on for the first time.

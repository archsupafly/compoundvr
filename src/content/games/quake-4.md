I strapped on my Quest, sideloaded the build, and pointed the barrel of the shotgun at a Strogg marine's chest. Twenty years after Quake 4 shipped, the kickback in my hands was the most alive that gun has ever felt. This isn't a window into the 2005 game. It's the game, rebuilt to live in your headset.

## What you're actually getting

There is no official VR mode for Quake 4. id Software never built one. What exists is a community conversion from Team Beef — the same crew behind the Quest ports of Doom 3, the Quake entries, Half-Life, and Return to Castle Wolfenstein. They call it Quake4Quest, and it's a native standalone VR build, not an injection driver pretending to be VR. You supply the game's asset files from a legally owned copy (Steam or GOG), sideload the APK, and you're in.

That last part matters for expectations. This is a Patreon-backed early-access build, distributed outside the Meta store. The gunplay, the movement, the world — those are real and running. But you are installing a beta, not downloading a finished product. If sideloading and community distribution are foreign to you, this is a step up in friction from a normal purchase. For anyone who's touched SideQuest before, it's a Tuesday.

VorpX has a Quake 4 profile too, but it's head-tracking-only with no motion controls — not a real alternative to what Team Beef built.

## How it plays

The port gives you full 6DOF and motion controllers. The railgun, the shotgun, the nailgun — they sit in your hands, and aiming down the barrel in a darkened Strogg corridor hits different than clicking a crosshair on a monitor. Movement is smooth locomotion, and the pace is the classic id Tech 4 sprint: fast, loud, and relentless. It's a moderately intense experience by VR standards, which is just the nature of a 2005 arena shooter — nothing about the port adds artificial sickness, it's the genre's speed doing what it's always done.

What surprised me is how well the engine holds up in the headset. id Tech 4's lighting — the sodium-lit corridors, the grimy industrial metal, the strobing emergency sections — reads with real depth when it's wrapped around your field of view. The atmosphere that made Quake 4 a moodier, grimmer cousin to Doom comes through cleanly when you're standing in it.

And then there's the moment the game earns its VR bones: the Strogg transformation. Without spoiling much, you get pulled into the enemy's conversion process, and experiencing that from inside the headset — the disorientation, the violation of it — lands harder than it ever did on a flat screen. That's the kind of beat VR was built to deliver, and the port doesn't fumble it.

## What's genuinely good

The headline is simple: this is a proper VR port of a game that never got one. Full motion controls, real presence, the entire campaign reachable from inside the headset. For a 2005 title running on standalone Quest hardware, it's efficient — the engine is light enough that it doesn't beg for a tethered PC, though Team Beef also ships playable PCVR builds if you'd rather push resolution and framerate on a rig.

The shooting is the draw. Quake 4's weapons have always been chunky and immediate, and in VR that weight translates. Clearing a room of Strogg with the shotgun feels like the game finally got the feedback it deserved two decades ago. The combat loop — push, reposition, unload, repeat — is exactly the kind of dumb fun that works great when your body is the aiming system.

## Where it stumbles

This is a beta-era community project, and it shows in the places betas show. Rough edges in menus, the occasional hitch, the understanding that you're playing something still being actively shaped. None of it broke my sessions, but I never forgot I was running an early build either.

The bigger caveat is the game itself. Quake 4 is a solid shooter, not a legendary one. The campaign is a competent corridor crawl with a sci-fi war story that does its job without ever reaching for something deeper. There's no sprawling world to inhabit, no RPG density, no reason to keep exploring once the credits roll. What you get is a tight, loud, satisfying shooter — and that's the whole package. If you go in expecting the world-building depth that pushes a VR port into the top tier, you'll come out wanting.

Acquisition is the other wrinkle. You need to own Quake 4, you need to sideload, and the build lives behind Team Beef's Patreon during this development window. That's a real gate for casual curiosity. This isn't a "try it on a whim" purchase — it's a project for someone who already has a copy gathering dust and a Quest ready to experiment.

## The bottom line

Quake4Quest is the real thing: a community conversion that respects the source material and delivers it in proper 6DOF with motion controls. It's a B-tier experience because the foundation is a good-but-not-great 2005 shooter and the delivery is a beta you have to assemble yourself — but the fun in the headset is undeniable. If you own Quake 4 and you're comfortable sideloading a community build, this is an easy yes for a weekend of Strogg-smashing. If you're looking for a polished store release or a world that'll swallow your free time, this isn't the portal you're hunting for.

I've lost count of the nights I spent in 15th-century Bohemia staring at a flat monitor, squinting at Henry's mud-caked boots and willing the world to feel closer than a screen allows. The KCD1VR mod is the first time that world has actually wrapped around me. Not a floating window, not a virtual desktop — real stereo depth and head tracking inside the original Kingdom Come: Deliverance.

## What the KCD1VR mod actually does

KCD1VR is a community Full VR Mod by farmerarmor that bolts native stereo OpenXR rendering and 6DOF head tracking onto the original game (version 1.9.7 on Steam). It's a proper stereoscopic conversion, not an injection trick: you're rendering two real eye views and looking around the world with your head, not watching a 3D-upconverted flat frame. It runs through the PCVR Mods Installer Hub, which handles per-eye resolution presets for ten headsets and wires up DLSS automatically, so most of the heavy lifting is done for you.

The catch that defines the whole experience is right in the mod's own README: motion controls are not implemented. You play with a gamepad or keyboard and mouse. There's no sword in your hand, no bow to draw — you trigger attacks and blocks through buttons while your head freely roams the scene. Before this mod, the only way into the headset was the paid vorpX injection driver, which delivered geometric 3D but no motion controls and a well-documented list of missing textures and a stubborn reticle.

## Standing inside Bohemia

This is where KCD1VR earns its keep. Kingdom Come: Deliverance is famous for its obsessive historical detail — the mud, the tabards, the way a village's politics actually matter — and that obsession pays off differently in VR than it ever did on a monitor. I walked into a tavern and found myself leaning in to read a notice pinned to the wall because that's what my body wanted to do, not because the game told me to. The scale of a courtyard, the cramped timber of a blacksmith's shop, the sightlines across an open field before a swordfight — all of it lands with the weight of place that flat screens flatten.

The presence is the point. You're not watching Henry's story; you're standing in the middle of it. The medieval world Warhorse built is exactly the kind of lived-in, story-driven setting that justifies a headset on its own, and the mod's 6DOF tracking lets you actually inhabit it rather than peer through a window.

## How it plays on a gamepad

Here's the honest tension: the head is free, the hands are not. Combat, lockpicking, and horseback riding all run through controller inputs, so the melee system that made Kingdom Come notorious — the timed directional swings, the stamina economy, the clinch — plays exactly as it did flat, just with your head untethered from the camera. That's a real loss. Half the magic of this game's fighting is the physical commitment; reducing it to stick-and-button inputs strips the sweat out of it.

But it works, and it works well enough that I kept playing. Menus and dialogue scenes intentionally drop to a flat, head-locked screen in front of you — expected behavior for a WIP conversion, and less jarring than it sounds once you accept that the world is the VR part and the UI is the flat part. The mod is WIP: current builds report occasional head-tracked latency and stutter on some Quest 3 setups, and early washed-out gamma was addressed in a later build via sRGB correction. None of it broke my sessions, but it's the kind of rough edge you should walk in expecting.

If the KCD1VR install gives you trouble, the vorpX injection profile still renders geometric 3D in the headset, though you'll trade away native stereo quality and pick up missing-texture and reticle quirks for your trouble.

## The details worth the headset

What surprised me most wasn't the big set pieces — it was the small stuff. Kingdom Come's world is built from readable, hand-made detail: the stitching on a nobleman's coat, the individual planks of a barn door, the way candlelight pools on a stone floor. On a monitor those details are wallpaper. In the headset they're objects with scale and weight, and the game's slow, observational pace finally makes sense. This is a game about standing in a place and noticing it, and that's exactly the thing VR is good at.

The first-person framing helps more than I expected. Kingdom Come was always first-person on foot, so you're not fighting a third-person camera's transition into VR — you're simply where Henry is. Conversations put a real face at real distance in front of you. The open fields of Sassau and Rattay stretch to a horizon you can actually turn and take in.

## The ceiling you feel

The gamepad is the hard limit. Everything I described above — the presence, the detail — lives in your head and eyes, but your hands are invisible to the game. You'll never physically draw a bow or parry a strike; you'll press a button and watch Henry do it. For a game whose combat is built around physical timing and commitment, that's a genuine trade. It's the difference between acting in the swordfight and directing it.

The WIP status is the second limit. The mod is openly a work in progress, and the rough edges show: flat head-locked menus and dialogue, the occasional stutter some users report, the gamma issues that needed a later correction. None of it stopped me, but none of it is finished either. You're an early traveler on this road.

## Performance and the rig you'll want

Kingdom Come was a heavy CryEngine title on flat hardware and it stays heavy in VR — native stereo roughly doubles the rendering load, and the game's dense vegetation and draw distances don't forgive a weak GPU. Plan on a high-end PC to hold framerate with the headset's resolution pushed up; a mid-range rig will run it but you'll be turning settings down and accepting reprojection. The mod's installer auto-configures DLSS, which helps, but this is not a lightweight conversion.

## Should you put the headset on

If you've already sunk hours into Kingdom Come: Deliverance and loved the world, KCD1VR is an easy yes — it's the difference between remembering Bohemia and standing in it, and the mod's WIP state is exactly the kind of rough-edged community project worth supporting with your playtime. If you've never played it, this is a fine way in for the curious, provided you're not coming for sword-in-hand VR combat; you're coming for the place, and the place is the best part of the game anyway.

The honest line: this is a B-tier VR experience because the world is exceptional and the headset finally lets you live in it, but the gamepad-only control and unfinished edges keep it from being the transformative conversion a great flat game can become. It's a recommendation with caveats — the caveats being your hands, and the clock on the mod's development. For the right player, the Bohemia you can finally walk through is worth both.

## The first time the lights died

I was bent over a terminal, typing out a moon route with my actual fingers, when the facility power cut and the world went black except for the faint red glow of my flashlight. Something heavy started moving in the room next to me. My friend's voice cracked over proximity chat: "Don't. Move." I didn't. I held my breath in real life. That is the difference between watching Lethal Company and being inside it.

Lethal Company has been a runaway hit on PC — a co-op scrap-scavenging horror game where you and three friends rob hostile moons for scrap, hoping the quota gets met before something eats you. On a monitor it's funny and tense. In VR, via the community LCVR mod, it becomes a physical thing: you reach for the shovel, you pull the lever, you cover your own mouth to muffle a cough when the monster is ten feet away.

## What LCVR actually is

LCVR is a BepInEx mod that bolts full six-degrees-of-freedom VR onto Lethal Company. It runs on Unity's OpenXR plugin, so it works across the usual PCVR runtimes — SteamVR, Virtual Desktop, Oculus — without per-headset hacking. The author, DaXcess, has kept it in lockstep with the game's frequent updates, which matters more than it sounds: Lethal Company patches often, and a mod that falls behind turns into a launcher error. This one hasn't.

You get it through the normal mod channels — Thunderstore via a manager like Gale, or a one-click PCVR Mods Installer Hub tile that handles the BepInEx wiring for you. Once it's in, you launch the game normally and the headset takes over. No separate executable, no injection profile to babysit.

That's the good news. The catch is version matching: because the game updates so often, you need the LCVR build that tracks your current Lethal Company version. When they drift, the mod's own listing flags a fallback. It's a manageable step, not a hobby, but it is the one bit of friction between you and the headset.

## Your hands are finally part of the joke

The reason this mod works better than most flat-to-VR conversions is that Lethal Company is built on dumb, tactile interactions, and LCVR hands them to you literally. You don't press a key to "use" the ship's lever — you grab it and shove it. You don't hit a button to charge a battery; you pick the battery up and slot it into the charger. You hold a walkie-talkie up to your face to talk. You cover your own mouth with your hand to stay quiet. You swing the shovel with both hands.

That last one is where the game lives. Combat in Lethal Company is mostly frantic, improvised violence — bonk the creature, run, bonk again — and doing it with a real overhead swing instead of a mouse-click is the single biggest upgrade over flat. The game's comedy comes from physics: a friend yeeted across the room by a stray shovel arc, a jetpack misfire that launches you into a wall. In VR those moments land in your body, not just on screen.

The controls are rebindable, and the defaults are sane: left stick moves and clicks to sprint, A jumps, right trigger interacts, the D-pad opens your inventory, B flips the flashlight. The in-game terminal uses a virtual keyboard you type on, and menus use ray interactors — point and pull the trigger. None of it fights you once you've spent ten minutes in the facility.

## The horror hits different in the dark

Lethal Company's scares are about sound and uncertainty: a thump in a room you can't see, a quota deadline, a friend who went quiet on the radio. VR strips away the safety of a framed screen. When the facility lights die and your only light is the narrow cone of your flashlight, the space around you is actually around you. The monsters — the ones that only move when you look away, the ones that mimic your friends' voices — prey on exactly the part of your brain VR amplifies.

This is also why comfort matters here in a way it doesn't for a bright arcade shooter. It's a first-person, smooth-locomotion horror game with no teleport option, so if sustained scary movement in a headset already turns your stomach, this one will too. For most VR players it sits at Moderate Intensity — you feel it, you play anyway. I'm not going to pretend the genre is gentle.

## Where the seams show

LCVR is a community mod of an early-access game that changes constantly, and that's the honest ceiling. When Lethal Company ships an update that LCVR hasn't matched yet, you're stuck on a previous build or waiting for a patch — the version-matching chore I mentioned isn't theoretical, it's the regular tax of playing a live service game through a third party. The flat-screen terminal, designed for a keyboard, is awkward in VR even with the virtual keyboard; you'll type routes slower than your flat friends and they will mock you for it.

Performance is moderate. This isn't a heavy UEVR injection with a 30 percent overhead tax — it's a Unity mod rendering natively through OpenXR — but Lethal Company's physics-heavy moments (four players, a hoard of scrap, a monster ragdolling someone across a room) can still dip on a mid-range PC. You don't need a supercomputer, but you shouldn't expect a potato to hold 90Hz through a panic spike. The lack of an official VR mode also means there's no developer-backed comfort menu or VR-native settings screen; what the mod gives you is what you get.

## The friends are the point

Here's the thing that lifts this above a clever tech demo: LCVR plays seamlessly with flat players. Your headset buddy and your monitor buddy share the same lobby, and non-VR clients with the mod installed actually see your head and hands moving in space — so when you dramatically cover your mouth or wind up a shovel swing, they watch you do it for real. The co-op panic that makes Lethal Company special isn't diminished by the mod; it's the entire reason to run it.

That said, this is a co-op game at its core. The loop — take a mission, scavenge, survive, hit quota — is thinner alone, and VR doesn't invent a solo campaign where none exists. The world is a backdrop for the chaos you and your friends cause, not a story you inhabit. For a horror title that's the right call, but it means the experience is only as good as the people you rope in.

## Grab the shovel

Lethal Company in VR is the rare mod that doesn't just make a flat game playable in a headset — it makes the flat version feel like a downgrade. Real hands on the shovel, real darkness around you, real screaming in your ear. The version-matching friction is real and the terminal is clumsy, but neither comes close to undoing how much fun this is with the right crew. If you already own the game and a PCVR headset, the install is the only thing standing between you and the most physical co-op horror on the platform. Bring friends who laugh when they die, and go get the scrap.

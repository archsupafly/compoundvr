There is a moment in *Five Nights at Freddy's VR: Help Wanted* when you realize the mask in your virtual hand isn't just for show. You have to pull it up, hold your breath, and wait while something with too many teeth and not enough lubricant stands inches from your face. It is a cheap horror trick, executed with the kind of physical immediacy that only VR can deliver, and I absolutely fell for it every single time.

This game is not a flat game that got patched into VR. It is a native VR title from Steel Wool Studios and Scott Cawthon, built in Unreal Engine 4, released on May 28, 2019 for PCVR and PSVR. That distinction matters because everything about the design assumes you are standing or sitting inside a dark room with motion controllers. The VR version is the game. The flat mode — added as a free update in December 2019 — is the concession, not the core.

## What It Actually Is

*Help Wanted* is an anthology of minigames drawn from the first six years of Five Nights at Freddy's. You get simplified VR recreations of *FNaF 1* through *Sister Location*, plus original challenges built specifically for the format. The mechanics are deliberately simple: watch cameras, close doors, manage power, repair systems, hide. The twist is that you do it with your actual hands in a 3D space, and the animatronics are now life-sized.

The physicality is what sells it. Pulling a lever, turning a key, pressing a button, yanking a mask over your eyes — these are small interactions, but they force you to be present. You can't look away as easily as you can on a monitor. The jumpscares, which would be routine on a flat screen, become genuinely startling because the thing lunging at you is doing it from your actual peripheral vision.

Comfort is better than you might expect. Most minigames are stationary or seated-friendly. There is no free locomotion to trigger motion sickness, no sprinting, no artificial turning to fight with. The intensity comes from atmosphere and surprise, not from your stomach. The only caveat is the PSVR2 *Full Time Edition*, which lists a 2m × 2m roomscale requirement. Even there, the actual gameplay is still mostly static.

## The Platform Question

Because this is a native VR game across multiple platforms, the real decision is where to buy it.

**PCVR** is the reference version. Buy it on Steam, launch it, and it just works with any SteamVR/OpenXR headset. Visual fidelity is the highest here, load times are short, and mod support exists if you want community content. If you want the cleanest, sharpest version and you already have a PCVR setup, this is it.

**Quest** is the most accessible version. Released on July 16, 2020, it runs standalone with no PC, no cable, no fuss. The graphics are pared down compared to PCVR — lower texture detail, simpler lighting, smaller environmental flourishes — but the core scare design is identical. For a game this reliant on audio cues and timing, the downgrade barely matters. This is the version most people end up playing, and it is hard to argue against it.

**PSVR2** is the best console version, released as the *Full Time Edition* on November 21, 2023. It includes the *Curse of Dreadbear* DLC and benefits from the headset's modern displays, haptics, and tracking. Setup is as plug-and-play as PSVR2 gets. The roomscale requirement is technically listed, but the game rarely asks you to move around much. If you are a console-first VR owner, this is the version to get.

**PSVR** on PS4 is still playable but firmly legacy. Tracking, resolution, and controller limitations make it the weakest official VR option. Only worth considering if you never moved to PSVR2.

**PICO** got the game on October 19, 2023, and functions as a Quest equivalent for PICO owners. It is viable, but the audience is smaller and the store ecosystem is less visible to most readers.

## What Holds It Back

The biggest weakness is repetition. The minigames are mechanically thin by design, and after a few hours the structure starts to feel familiar. Survive the night, unlock the next challenge, repeat. The horror works because of immediacy, not depth, and that immediacy can wear thin if you try to marathon it.

Platform parity is also uneven. The Quest port is a real technical compromise, and while the scares survive, the atmosphere takes a visible hit. The PSVR2 port is much stronger, but it arrived four and a half years after the original — a long wait for a console audience. None of this breaks the game, but it explains why *Help Wanted* lands at **B-tier** instead of **A**: the VR implementation is excellent, but the game underneath is a collection of short, similar experiences rather than a world you want to live in.

## Mods, Injectors, and Why You Can Ignore Them

There is no meaningful "flat-to-VR" route here because the game is already VR. UEVR could technically inject the non-VR version, but that would be a deliberately worse version of an experience that already exists natively. VorpX does not list the game among supported titles, and even if it did, there would be no reason to use it. Community mods like *Help Wanted +* are content expansions for people who already own the native VR version, not a way to enable VR. Emulators are simply not applicable.

If you are researching how to play this game in VR, the answer is: buy the native version for whatever headset you own. That is the whole guide.

## The Bottom Line

*Five Nights at Freddy's VR: Help Wanted* is the best argument for why horror and VR belong together. It is not the deepest game, and it is not the prettiest on every platform, but it understands exactly what makes VR scary: scale, proximity, and the fact that you cannot look away without physically turning your head. For anyone who owns a VR headset and can handle a jump scare, it is an easy recommendation. Just pick the native version for your platform and don't waste time trying to mod a flat game that already does the job better itself.

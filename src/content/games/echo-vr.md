I've thrown a lot of virtual discs in my life. But the first time I pushed off a curved arena wall in Echo VR and genuinely lost track of which way was down, I got why people called this the purest sport VR ever produced. There is no floor. There is no up. There is you, a disc, and three teammates trying to put that disc through a goal on the other side of a zero-gravity bowl while the other team tries to snap your spine with a plasma lance.

Echo VR is native VR through and through — Ready At Dawn built it for the Rift in 2017, free to play, and later brought the entire package to Quest as a standalone build. There was never a flat version. This is VR from the ground up: two modes, Arena and Combat, both played in the same weightless arena. Arena is ultimate Frisbee with the rules stripped for speed — grab the disc, boost across the map, bank it off a wall into the goal. Combat bolts a hero-shooter on top: an Energy Lance for melee, a sidearm, ordinance, and a respawn timer.

The genius is the movement. You don't walk. You grab a surface — a wall, a teammate, the disc — and push off, then you're gliding on momentum until you grab something else or fire a short boost from your wrist. After ten minutes it stops being a control scheme and starts being a body. You tuck, you spin, you drift past an opponent who committed to the wrong vector, and you realize your actual reflexes are doing the aiming. Throwing the disc is the same trick: wind up with your whole arm, release with your wrist, and it curves with real spin. Nobody explained the curve to me. I felt it.

Combat tightens the screws. The lance is a melee poke that rewards closing distance in a space where distance is a lie. The gun fights you — recoil in zero-g means you drift when you fire, so spraying is suicide and a single clean shot from across the arena is the whole game. The wrist-mounted UI is the only flat thing in the experience, and even that you call up by twisting your arm, not by clicking a menu.

Here's the thing: this is VR doing something no monitor ever could. Zero-gravity full-body movement with motion controls isn't a gimmick bolted onto a flat game — it's the game. The presence is the point. When a pass comes in hot and you have to rotate your whole torso to catch it mid-drift, your stomach believes you're floating. That's the bar every other VR sport gets measured against, and most don't clear it.

The rhythm of a good Arena match is its own reward. A clean outlet pass, a defender biting on a fake, the disc rattling off the inside of the goal — these are moments you earned with your arms, not a thumbstick. It's free to pick up on the hardware it shipped for, the skill ceiling is absurd, and the best matches feel like a real team sport: callouts, rotations, the quiet dread of an opponent breaking for your goal unprotected. When it clicks, there is nothing else like it on the platform.

Look, I'm not gonna lie about the one thing that defines this title: it is live-service multiplayer, and the official servers are gone. Meta shut them down. The reason you can still play is a community project — EchoRelay — that rebuilds the backend and runs dedicated servers, with installers that get the client onto both PCVR and Quest. It works: Arena and Combat both run, public and private matches, social lobby, the lot. But you'll do some setup (sideload the client, point it at a community server, coordinate matches through Discord), the population is a fraction of the official peak, you can't party up in squads the way the old system allowed, and AI matches are thin on the current build. You're relying on people who love this game enough to keep the lights on.

Zero-g free flight can disorient at first — there's a comfort vignette if you want it, but most VR veterans adapt within a session.

If you want a single reason to own a VR headset, Echo VR has been one of them since launch, and the community has refused to let it die. The experience in the headset is exceptional: full motion controls, genuine zero-g presence, a sport that only exists because VR exists. The caveats are real — you need the community path to play, and you need other people in the arena to feel it. But if you can find a match, and through EchoRelay you can, put the headset on. This is still the one to show people what VR movement can be.

The first time I booted Cyberpilot, I expected to be the Nazi-killing machine. Instead I spent the opening ten minutes in a chair, pulling a lever to ride an elevator between floors of a Resistance bunker, reaching out to tighten a bolt on a robot dog. That's the whole game in miniature: Wolfenstein: Cyberpilot is about remote-piloting the war, not fighting it. You're a hacker in 1980s Paris, and when the shooting starts, you're watching through the eyes of the metal you've hijacked.

That distance is the thing you have to make peace with before you spend a cent.

## What it actually is

This is a VR-native spin-off from 2019, built by Arkane Lyon with MachineGames and published by Bethesda — no flat version exists, and it never needed a mod or an injector to get into a headset. It landed on PSVR and on PC through Steam on the same day, and it's still on both storefronts. On PC it runs through SteamVR and officially supports Vive, Windows Mixed Reality, and Valve Index; Oculus Rift and Rift S work through the OpenVR pipeline but Bethesda never gave them a stamp of approval, and the studio said outright at launch that support could break with a future patch. Quest owners have no native version — you'd be streaming the PC build. There's no PSVR2 edition either.

Setup is the easy part. Console players plug in a headset; PC players install and go. You aim with a controller stick on both platforms — PlayStation Move controllers add some reach-out interaction in the hub, but the combat aiming is stick-based, not hand-presence.

The campaign is four missions, three pilotable Nazi machines: the flamethrower Panzerhund, the heavy Zitadelle mech, and a small cloaking drone built for a stealth-hack level. Between missions you putter around the bunker. Total time is somewhere in the one-and-a-half-to-three-hour range depending on platform and how much you poke at the hub — I was done well before lunch.

## How it plays

Here's what surprised me: the presentation is genuinely good. For a 2019 VR title it looks sharp, and the cockpit framing — you seated, screens and controls around you — does a real job of grounding your stomach during movement. The drone level, where you cloak and slip past patrols to hack terminals, is the standout: it's the one stretch where the remote-piloting fantasy clicks and the game feels like something its bigger siblings couldn't do.

The rest is thinner than it looks. Combat is linear corridor shooting with the threat dialed low — enemies catch fire or short out and drop, and because you're piloting from afar, you never feel the weight of it. Reviewers and players landed on the same word: a shooting gallery. The machines handle fine, the shield mechanic is a nice panic button, but there's no real pushback, no reason to learn the systems, and nothing to come back for once the credits roll.

Comfort is the other honest asterisk, and it splits people harder than almost any VR title I've covered. The game gives you the right tools — smooth or snap turning with adjustable speed, an FOV dimmer, and a vignette you can dial or disable — and the cockpit helps. But the reports are weirdly bimodal: plenty of players cruise through, and a meaningful chunk describe the worst nausea they've felt in any headset, options or not. If smooth-locomotion FPS comfort is already a question mark for you, this is not the place to find out.

## What's worth your time

At full price this was a hard sell — twenty bucks for a couple of hours with no replay is a stretch. The current PlayStation Store price, deep in the single digits, changes the math. For that money, a Wolfenstein obsessive gets a short, stylish afternoon of piloting mechs through Paris, and the drone mission is a genuinely different flavor of the franchise. The visuals hold up, the premise is fun, and it runs on modest VR hardware (a GTX 1070-class card meets the minimum).

The cracks are just impossible to ignore. The remote-piloting conceit that makes it novel also keeps you outside the fantasy — you're not in Wolfenstein, you're watching it through a robot's eyes. The campaign ends right as it gets interesting, there's no multiplayer and no endgame, and the Rift crowd is always one patch away from a broken launch. It's also a dead-end product: no updates since launch, no Quest or PSVR2 port on the horizon, and Bethesda's VR push has moved on.

## Who this is for

If you're collecting every Wolfenstein in VR, or you want a cheap, low-commitment mech-piloting curiosity for an afternoon, it does the job — buy it on sale and don't expect more than it is. If you came here for the boots-on-ground, blood-on-the-walls Wolfenstein presence, this isn't that game, and no amount of flamethrower dogs will make the chair feel like the battlefield. It's a footnote to a better series, rendered in real 3D.

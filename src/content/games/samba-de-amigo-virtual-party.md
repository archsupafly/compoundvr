There's a specific kind of ridiculous joy in standing in your living room, holding two controllers like maracas, and absolutely committing to a Lady Gaga song at full volume. Samba de Amigo: Virtual Party is the first time SEGA's maraca-shaking rhythm series has come to VR — and it turns out the maraca fantasy was always a VR fantasy waiting to happen.

## Picking Up the Maracas

This is SEGA's first internally developed VR game, and they built it native for Quest. There's no PC involved, no sideloading, no mod installer, no compatibility rabbit hole. You buy it on the Horizon Store, you download it, and you're holding maracas. If you've got a Quest 2, Quest Pro, or Quest 3, it runs — the whole thing was designed around Touch controllers from day one.

That matters more than it sounds. A lot of rhythm games graft motion controls onto a flat foundation and hope the tracking holds. Samba doesn't have that problem, because shaking is the only input it has ever known. There is no gamepad path, and there doesn't need to be. You're either holding maracas or you're not playing.

## Six Rings, One Beat

Here's how a song actually goes. Six rings hang in space in front of you — high, mid, and low, mirrored left and right. Colored rhythm balls travel down those rings, and your job is to shake the matching controller in time as each ball reaches the target. Sometimes the game wants a pose instead: arms out, maracas up overhead, and you hold it steady. Sometimes it traces an arrow and you sweep your hand along the path like you're conducting. The reading is generous enough that commitment counts more than precision, which is the right call for a game built around feeling the beat in your shoulders.

The base library is 40-plus licensed tracks — Lady Gaga, Rina Sawayama, Ricky Martin, Bon Jovi — and SEGA keeps the lights on with paid song packs pulling from their own catalog: Sonic, Like a Dragon, Space Channel 5, Rhythm Thief. Beyond the quick-play rhythm mode there's StreamiGo!, a campaign wrapped around influencer-style missions, plus character customization and online leaderboards if you want to chase a number.

## Motion That Earns It

This is where the headset earns its keep. Shaking maracas in time is exactly the kind of full-body, both-hands physicality that flat rhythm games could only ever gesture at with a button prompt. The Quest 3 Touch Plus controllers give noticeably better haptic punch than the detached Joy-Cons of the Switch version, so when you crack off a clean hit the feedback lands in your hand. The tracking reads your shake, not your button press — the game judges movement, not timing on a face button. That presence, the sense that your own arms are the instrument, is the entire reason this works in VR and dies flat.

No comfort settings are needed — you plant your feet and shake in place, so there's nothing here to turn your stomach, and newcomers can stand or sit through the full game.

## Mixed Reality: A Bonus, Not the Point

Two of the modes — MR Party and MR Multiverse — use Quest 3 and Quest Pro passthrough to drop the rings into your actual room. It's a neat trick the first time your coffee table becomes part of the stage. But the mixed-reality modes feel gimmicky, and they mostly are a filter laid over the core game, not a reinvention of how you play. If you've got a Quest 3 it's a fun ten-minute novelty, and then you'll almost certainly flip back to the cleaner painted-world look.

## Where the Party Stalls

The caveats live in the multiplayer. World Party drops you into 20-player online knockout lobbies, which sounds like the series finally leaning into its party-game soul — except the lobbies sat empty enough that waits ran two to three minutes, and there's no way to pull four friends into a private room together. For a franchise whose entire identity is a living-room gathering, that's a real miss. You can fill a public lobby, but you can't host the party you pictured buying this for.

The mixed-reality modes are thinner than the marketing implies, as noted. And the licensed library leans hard into pop — if you came hoping for a deep, focused celebration of Latin and Hispanic music, the tracklist will feel lighter than the name promises. SEGA clearly aimed for broad playlist appeal, and the trade is that the samba heart of the series gets less room than the title suggests.

## Worth the Sweat

If you want a VR game that makes you move, laugh, and look vaguely absurd in front of your own mirror, this is an easy yes. The solo and StreamiGo rhythm experience is SEGA at its most playful — physical, generous, and built for the headset from the ground up rather than bent into it. The online party is the one place it stumbles, and if four-player living-room sessions with your friends were the whole reason you were interested, wait and hope SEGA patches in private lobbies.

For everyone else: pick up the maracas. The beat is waiting, your arms already know what to do, and the Quest was partly built for exactly this kind of foolish, joyful noise.

The first ball hit me square in the face, and the robot voice on the intercom said, deadpan, "That was not a header. That was a personal failure." I was in the headset, on a wooden bench in a concrete room that is absolutely not a prison, being retrained as a footballer by an AI with the comedic timing of a spiteful HR department. I laughed, reset my stance, and let the next ball bounce off my forehead with intent. Headmaster is the only game I've played where the core verb is literally moving your skull.

Headmaster launched as a PSVR title on October 13, 2016, and it's a native VR game through and through — there is no flatscreen version, no monitor mode, no "VR optional" checkbox. You can't play it without a headset because the entire game is about your head being the controller. Frame Interactive followed it to PC VR on August 30, 2017, with native SteamVR and Oculus Rift support, so however you're set up, there's a way in. This isn't a mod, an injector, or a port with the VR bolted on after the fact — it was built for the headset from the first line of code.

**What you're actually getting**

The setup is the easy end of VR. On PSVR you need the headset, the camera, and a DualShock 4; it's plug-and-play. On PC you need a SteamVR headset (Vive, Rift, Index, whatever you've got) and the game runs natively through Steam — no tinkering, no profile hunting. The PC version even adds a few things the PS4 original doesn't: a Practice Zone, a Stadium Level, and a "No Hands" mode where you play the whole thing with head movement alone, no controller at all. Both versions share the same campaign and the same local party mode, where up to six people pass the headset around and take turns while everyone else heckles from the couch.

**How it plays**

You stand or sit in place and move your head to meet incoming balls. Soccer balls first, then the game escalates into outright absurdity: explosive balls, knife balls, dart balls, beach balls, garbage bags. Each level asks you to hit a target, clear a pattern, or survive a wave, and the snarky instructor narrates your disgrace in real time. The physicality is the whole point — you're not pressing a button to "head" a ball, you're literally leaning, ducking, and snapping your neck to connect. Comfort is a non-issue: you're stationary, there's no locomotion, and the only thing moving is your head, which is exactly the muscle the game is exercising.

The comedy is what carries it. The "Football Improvement Centre — which is not a prison" framing is a sustained Portal-vs-Wii-Sports bit, and it lands more than it should. You graduate through levels, get praised, get demoted, head a giant monkey, and slowly realize the place has no intention of letting you leave. The writing is the reason this isn't just a tech demo — it gives the repetitive act of heading balls a reason to keep going.

**What's genuinely good**

The headline strength is that this is a VR-native concept done right. Heading balls with your actual head feels like nothing else on the platform, and the satisfaction of reading a ball's trajectory and meeting it flush is real, physical fun. The campaign runs 5 to 7 hours across 40-plus levels, which is a genuinely generous length for a VR launch title — this isn't a ninety-minute tease. The ball variety keeps the mechanics from going stale: destruction balls, precision darts, and pure-comedy beach balls each ask your neck to do something different. And the local party mode is the sleeper — pass-and-play with a group turns a solo physics toy into a genuinely funny room experience. It's the rare VR game that works as a living-room social thing, not just a solo one.

**Where it's thin**

The honest caveat is the concept is one joke, and the joke is the game. You head balls. That's the verb, start to finish, and while the writing dresses it up, the underlying loop doesn't deepen the way a puzzle game's systems might. Later levels swap ball types more than they add ideas, and the "40+ levels" can read as the same challenge reskinned rather than forty genuinely new thoughts. If the comedy doesn't land for you, there's not much underneath to hold you — it's a funny idea executed well, not a deep one. Frame Interactive later added *The Lost Lessons*, a DLC pack of extra levels, which is a fine excuse to come back if you finished the base game and wanted more of the same — but it's more of the same by design.

**Bottom line**

Headmaster is the kind of game that only exists because VR exists, and it uses the headset for something you can't do on a monitor. The head-tracking physicality is novel, the comedy is real, and the campaign is longer than the premise implies. Get it if you want a funny, physical VR-native game that shows off what head presence actually does — and grab it knowing the one great idea is the whole game. Skip it if you need depth under your comedy, because what you see in the first ten minutes is the entire pitch.
